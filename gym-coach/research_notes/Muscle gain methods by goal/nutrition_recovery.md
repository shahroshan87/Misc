# Nutrition, supplementation and recovery for muscle gain in a lean "hardgainer" man (worked example: 38-year-old South Asian, skinny, wants to get visibly bigger)

> Method note for the report writer: research done 2026-11-17. Direct page fetches to pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov were blocked by the session network policy, so every figure below was confirmed from search-result renderings of abstracts / bibliographic records, not from full text. Each finding is tagged **[abstract]** (number seen in the paper's abstract or an institutional record of it) or **[secondary]** (number seen only in a news/blog summary of the paper; verify before publishing). Figures I could not confirm at all are listed under Gaps, not as findings.

## 1. Energy surplus: how large, and what rate of weight gain?

### Takeaway
The surplus needed for muscle gain is not precisely known; the best current evidence supports a small surplus (roughly 5-20% above maintenance, or about 350-480 kcal/day as a starting point) with body weight rising about 0.25-0.5% per week for novices/intermediates and slower for advanced lifters. Larger surpluses in controlled trials mainly added fat, not extra muscle.

### Cited Findings
- Slater et al. 2019 (Frontiers in Nutrition 6:131) conclude that "the specific energy surplus required to facilitate muscle hypertrophy is unknown" and that textbook estimates based only on the energy stored in new tissue have never been validated in a resistance-training population. **[abstract]** — [Slater et al. 2019, Front Nutr](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/full)
- Slater et al. 2019 recommend starting with a conservative surplus of 1,500-2,000 kJ/day (converted as 358-478 kcal/day) and adjusting from body-weight/body-composition changes. **[secondary]** — [BarBend summary of Slater 2019](https://barbend.com/how-many-calories-to-eat-bulking-season)
- Iraki, Fitschen, Espinar & Helms 2019 (Sports 7(7):154): "A hyper-energetic diet (~10-20%) should be consumed with a target weight gain of ~0.25-0.5% of bodyweight/week for novice/intermediate bodybuilders. Advanced bodybuilders should be more conservative with the caloric surplus and weekly weight gain." **[abstract]** — [Iraki et al. 2019, Sports](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/7/7/154); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31247944/)
- Helms et al. 2023 (Sports Medicine - Open 9:102) randomised 21 trained lifters (17 completers) to maintenance, moderate (5%) or high (15%) energy surplus for 8 weeks of resistance training 3 days/week. Skinfold thickness rose more in the 15% group than at maintenance (moderate Bayesian evidence); there was no evidence for group differences in muscle thickness or squat 1-RM; bench 1-RM rose more in the 15% group. Body-mass change strongly predicted skinfold change but only weakly predicted muscle-thickness change. Authors' conclusion: faster rates of body-mass gain (larger surpluses) "primarily increase rates of fat gain rather than augmenting 1-RM or muscle thickness". Authors flag mixed training experience and moderate training volume as limits. **[abstract]** — [Helms et al. 2023, Sports Med Open](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37914977/)
- Garthe et al. 2013 (Eur J Sport Sci 13(3):295-303): 39 elite athletes randomised to a counselled meal plan with positive energy balance (n=21) vs ad libitum eating (n=18), all doing 4 strength sessions/week. Body weight rose 3.9% vs 1.5%; fat mass rose 15±4% vs 3±3%; lean body mass gain did not differ between groups. Authors advise that excess energy in a weight-gain protocol "should be considered carefully due to undesirable increases in body fat". **[abstract]** — [Garthe et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23679146/)
- A 2026 preprint discussed by Biolayne estimates the direct energy cost of building muscle at roughly 41 kcal/day and cautions against treating that as a surplus target. **[secondary, preprint, not peer reviewed]** — [Biolayne REPS issue 53](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-53/how-many-calories-does-it-actually-take-to-build-muscle/)

### Inferences
- A defensible app default for a novice hardgainer: start at maintenance + ~10-20% (or roughly +350-480 kcal/day), then steer by the scale: aim for ~0.25-0.5% of body weight per week. Arithmetic illustration only (hypothetical 60 kg user; the worked example's real weight was not given): 0.15-0.30 kg/week, about 0.6-1.3 kg/month.
- Rule logic the evidence supports: if the 2-3 week weight trend is below the band, add calories; if above the band, cut back, because extra surplus mostly becomes fat (Helms 2023, Garthe 2013).
- Novices sit at the top of the band and advanced lifters at the bottom or below (Iraki 2019). For a truly underweight novice the top of the band is reasonable; no trial found here tested underweight novices specifically, so this is extrapolation.
- Both key trials (Helms 2023, Garthe 2013) used trained people or elite athletes. Novices have more room to grow, so the "extra surplus = only fat" result may be less harsh for a skinny beginner, but there is no direct trial evidence in this notes file showing that a large surplus builds more muscle in novices.

### Gaps
- No RCT located that tests surplus size specifically in underweight/novice "hardgainer" men. Slater 2019 states the required surplus is unknown.
- I could not open the Slater 2019 full text, so the 1,500-2,000 kJ/day figure rests on a secondary summary (it matches my recollection of the paper, but verify at doi 10.3389/fnut.2019.00131).
- The often-quoted Rozenek et al. 2002 novice overfeeding study (large surplus plus training producing several kg of fat-free mass in 8 weeks) was not retrieved; I give no numbers for it.
- No South Asian-specific surplus trial found.

## 2. Protein: daily target, per-meal distribution, higher intakes, plant-based diets

### Takeaway
About 1.6 g/kg/day is the evidence-based floor for maximising training-induced lean mass in adults under 65, with 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day the usual practical range, split into 3-6 feedings of about 0.25-0.55 g/kg. Matched for total protein at 1.6-2.0 g/kg/day, vegan/plant-based diets produced the same muscle gains as omnivorous diets in two trials, but both relied on large amounts of concentrated plant protein (soy isolate, mycoprotein).

### Cited Findings
- Morton et al. 2018 (Br J Sports Med 52:376-384): meta-analysis of 49 RCTs, 1,863 participants, resistance training ≥6 weeks. Protein supplementation added +0.30 kg fat-free mass (95% CI 0.09 to 0.52) and +2.49 kg 1-RM strength. Intakes above ~1.6 g/kg/day (record gives 1.62) produced no further fat-free-mass gain. Effect was larger in resistance-trained people (0.75 kg) and absent in older people (-0.01 kg). **[abstract]** — [Morton et al. 2018, BJSM](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/); [McMaster record](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/763612)
- Nunes et al. 2022 (J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle 13(2):795-810): meta-analysis of 74 RCTs in healthy non-obese adults. A significant lean-body-mass effect appeared in adults <65 doing resistance training who ate ≥1.6 g/kg/day (for ≥65 years, at 1.2-1.59 g/kg/day). Lower-body strength gains were slightly larger at ≥1.6 g/kg/day. Overall: higher protein gives "small" additional gains; evidence certainty moderate for lean mass, low for several strength outcomes. **[abstract]** — [Nunes et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35187864/)
- ISSN position stand on protein (Jäger et al. 2017, JISSN 14:20): 1.4-2.0 g/kg/day is sufficient for most exercising individuals to build and maintain muscle; 2.3-3.1 g/kg/day may help retain lean mass in resistance-trained people during hypocaloric periods (i.e. dieting, not bulking). **[abstract]** — [Jäger et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)
- ISSN protein stand, per-meal guidance: about 0.25 g/kg of high-quality protein or an absolute 20-40 g per serving; doses should contain 700-3,000 mg leucine; spread evenly every 3-4 h; 30-40 g casein before sleep increases overnight muscle protein synthesis. **[secondary for the 0.25 g/kg and leucine figures; abstract for pre-sleep casein]** — [Jäger et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/); [NutraIngredients summary](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2017/06/21/ISSN-publishes-position-stand-on-protein-and-exercise/)
- Iraki et al. 2019: "Sufficient protein (1.6-2.2 g/kg/day) should be consumed with optimal amounts 0.40-0.55 g/kg per meal and distributed evenly throughout the day (3-6 meals)." **[abstract]** — [Iraki et al. 2019](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/7/7/154)
- Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018 (JISSN 15:10): spreading the upper daily intake of 2.2 g/kg/day over four meals gives a maximum of 0.55 g/kg/meal; they argue against a hard 20-25 g per-meal ceiling. **[abstract/full-text excerpt]** — [Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430)
- Trommelen et al. 2023 (Cell Reports Medicine): 100 g vs 25 g milk protein after resistance exercise gave a larger and more sustained anabolic response over 12 h; authors conclude the anabolic response "has no apparent upper limit in magnitude". Critics note participants were recreationally active, no intermediate dose was tested, and acute protein synthesis is not a proxy for hypertrophy. **[secondary]** — [Peter Attia summary](https://peterattiamd.com/protein-anabolic-responses/)
- Hevia-Larraín et al. 2021 (Sports Medicine 51:1317-1330): 19 habitual vegans vs 19 omnivores, young men, 12 weeks supervised resistance training twice weekly, protein set to 1.6 g/kg/day using soy isolate (vegans) or whey (omnivores). Both groups gained about 1.2 kg leg lean mass with no between-group differences in lean mass, fibre cross-sectional area or leg-press 1-RM (all p>0.05). Vegans needed about 58 g/day supplemental soy protein vs about 41 g/day whey in omnivores to reach target. Authors caution the result applies to healthy young adults on a vegan diet with sizeable supplemental isolate. **[abstract + press release]** — [Hevia-Larraín et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01434-9); [McMaster record](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1960616); [FAPESP release](https://agencia.fapesp.br/35898)
- Monteyne et al. 2023 (J Nutr 153(6):1680-1695): Phase 1, 16 young adults at 1.8 g/kg/day for 3 days, omnivorous vs exclusively non-animal protein: daily myofibrillar protein synthesis did not differ. Phase 2, 22 young adults, 10 weeks high-volume resistance training at about 2 g/kg/day, omnivorous (n=12) vs vegan (n=10, mycoprotein-based): thigh muscle volume +8.3% in both; fibre CSA +33±24% vs +32±48%; lean mass +2.6±1.1 kg vs +3.1±2.5 kg (P>0.05). Small samples; fibre intake roughly double in the vegan group. **[abstract]** — [Monteyne et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36822394/)
- Leucine content is described as the strongest determinant of a protein's capacity to stimulate muscle protein synthesis, with whey particularly high, in a 2016 Phillips-led review (part-funded by the Whey Protein Research Consortium). **[secondary; industry-funded]** — [NutraIngredients coverage](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2016/10/27/protein-quality-matters-for-muscle-building-says-review/)
- Safety: Devries et al. 2018 (J Nutr) pooled 28 studies, 1,358 participants without chronic kidney disease; high-protein diets (≥1.5 g/kg/day, or ≥20% energy, or ≥100 g/day) did not adversely change GFR; authors conclude high protein intakes "do not adversely influence kidney function on GFR in healthy adults". Main limitation: unclear selection-bias risk; some authors have industry disclosures. **[abstract + press]** — [McMaster record](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1607295); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/nephrology/20190225/highprotein-diets-do-not-affect-gfr-in-healthy-individuals)
- Contested position: ICMR-NIN Dietary Guidelines for Indians 2024 set the protein RDA at 0.83 g/kg/day (EAR 0.66 g/kg/day), state that intakes above ~1.6 g/kg/day add no further resistance-training gains, and advise against protein supplements for building body mass, citing possible bone-mineral loss and kidney damage with prolonged large intakes of protein powders. **[secondary: news reports of the guideline]** — [ThePrint](https://theprint.in/india/icmr-calls-for-avoiding-protein-supplements-issues-dietary-guidelines-for-indians/2077477); [The South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/health/icmr-releases-dietary-guidelines-for-indians-after-13-years-says-no-to-protein-supplements/). This conflicts with Devries 2018 (no GFR harm in healthy adults) and the ISSN stand; both sides agree on ~1.6 g/kg/day as the point of diminishing returns.

### Inferences
- App target: 1.6 g/kg/day as the floor, 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day as the range; going higher is not harmful to healthy kidneys per Devries 2018 but adds little for muscle (Morton 2018, Nunes 2022). Arithmetic illustration for a hypothetical 60 kg user: 96-132 g/day, in 3-6 feedings of about 24-33 g (0.40-0.55 g/kg).
- Distribution is second-order: total daily protein drives the result (Morton 2018; ISSN). A simple rule ("protein at every meal, 3-5 times a day") captures most of the benefit.
- For lacto-vegetarians (dairy available) the plant-vs-animal question is easier than for vegans: milk, curd, paneer and whey are high-quality proteins. For mostly-legume/cereal eaters, the two parity trials suggest aiming for the same or slightly higher total (toward 1.8-2.0 g/kg/day, the intake used in Monteyne 2023) and using soy or a protein powder to get there, because parity was shown only with concentrated protein sources.
- The 38-year-old worked example is under 65, so the "<65 years" thresholds apply.
- Anyone with known kidney disease, a single kidney, or diabetes with kidney involvement should get a doctor's input before a high-protein diet; the safety data are from people without kidney disease.

### Gaps
- Morton 2018's upper confidence limit for the breakpoint (widely quoted as about 2.2 g/kg/day, the basis of the "1.6-2.2" range) was not visible in the records I could access; verify in the paper before quoting it.
- Schoenfeld & Aragon's lower per-meal figure (0.4 g/kg across at least four meals) was not surfaced directly; the 0.40-0.55 g/kg range is confirmed via the Iraki 2019 abstract.
- Exact leucine percentages by protein source (commonly quoted about 10-11% for whey vs 6-8% for most plant proteins) were found only on retailer/blog pages with no primary citation; not reported as findings.
- No trial found of muscle gain on a typical lacto-vegetarian South Asian diet (dal/roti/rice/dairy) without supplemental protein isolates.
- I could not read the ICMR-NIN 2024 guideline document itself; its wording is from news coverage.

## 3. Protein intake in South Asian / Indian diets and practical high-protein foods

### Takeaway
Average Indian protein intake is about 62-63 g per person per day, with 40-48% coming from cereals, so intake is adequate against the 0.83 g/kg RDA but well short of a 1.6 g/kg muscle-gain target and of lower quality (lysine-limited). Reaching the target on a vegetarian South Asian diet realistically needs deliberate use of dairy, pulses, soy and/or protein powder; eggs and chicken make it much easier.

### Cited Findings
- National survey (MoSPI Household Consumption Expenditure Survey, "Nutritional Intake in India", press note 2 July 2025): per-capita protein intake was 61.9 g/day rural and 63.2 g/day urban in 2022-23, and 61.8 g rural / 63.4 g urban in 2023-24 (up from 59.3 g rural / 58.8 g urban in 2009-10). **[secondary: news reports of the MoSPI release]** — [MoSPI press note](https://mospi.gov.in/sites/default/files/press_release/press_note_Nutritional%20Intake%20in%20India_02072025.pdf); [Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/latest/economy/story/indians-eating-better-as-consumption-expenditure-rises-nso-report-482442-2025-06-30); [Rural Voice](https://eng.ruralvoice.in/calories-without-nutrition-new-survey-reveals-quiet-dietary-crisis-in-india)
- Cereals remain the largest protein source: reported as 46-48% of protein intake in rural India and about 39-40.6% in urban India in 2023-24 (two reports give slightly different figures). **[secondary]** — [Rural Voice](https://eng.ruralvoice.in/calories-without-nutrition-new-survey-reveals-quiet-dietary-crisis-in-india); [The South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/health/full-plates-empty-calories-south-indias-nutrition-paradox-defies-prosperity/)
- Swaminathan, Vaz & Kurpad 2012 (Br J Nutr, "Protein intakes in India"): Indian diets derive about 60% of protein from cereals (57-70% in rural/tribal groups), which are of relatively low digestibility and quality; scored by PDCAAS with lysine as the first limiting amino acid, protein quality appears inadequate in all population groups studied, especially rural and tribal; intakes in disadvantaged groups were about 1 g/kg/day. **[abstract]** — [St John's Research Institute record](https://www.stjohns.in/publications/protein-intakes-in-india); [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E4D9E34413FA06FF57844888FC6084C3/core-reader)
- ICMR-NIN RDA for protein is 0.83 g/kg/day (EAR 0.66 g/kg/day); the 2024 guideline's model 2,000 kcal plate includes 85 g of pulses/eggs/flesh foods. **[secondary]** — [The South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/health/icmr-releases-dietary-guidelines-for-indians-after-13-years-says-no-to-protein-supplements/)
- ICMR-NIN 2024 notes vegetarians have difficulty getting enough vitamin B12 and n-3 fatty acids and recommends n-3-rich seeds (flax, chia). **[secondary]** — [The South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/health/icmr-releases-dietary-guidelines-for-indians-after-13-years-says-no-to-protein-supplements/)
- Protein per 100 g, Indian Food Composition Tables 2017 values as quoted by secondary pages (exact page-to-number mapping not verified; check against the IFCT 2017 document before publishing): paneer 18.86 g (food code L003); soya chunks, dry about 52 g; toor/red gram dal, dry about 22 g; moong/green gram dal, dry about 24-24.5 g; masoor/red lentil, dry about 24-25 g; egg, boiled 13.43 g; curd about 3.1 g. **[secondary]** — [InstaCuppa paneer page](https://instacuppastore.com/blogs/main-page/paneer-protein-exact-grams-per-100g-per-slice-and-per-cube); [InstaCuppa dal page](https://instacuppastore.com/blogs/main-page/dal-protein-which-dal-has-the-most-protein-full-guide-for-indians); [Organic Mandya dal comparison](https://www.organicmandya.com/know-your-food/pulses-and-dals/dal-protein-comparison/)
- Protein per 100 g, USDA FoodData Central values as mirrored by aggregator pages: lentils, boiled 9.0 g (raw 24.6 g); chickpeas, cooked 8.86 g (raw about 20.5 g); whole egg 12.6 g (raw or hard-boiled); chicken breast, roasted 31.0 g (raw skinless 22.5 g); tofu, firm, calcium-set 17.3 g (FDC 172475). **[secondary mirror of USDA]** — [Nutrition Advance table](https://www.nutritionadvance.com/protein-content-of-common-foods); [USDA FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/)

### Inferences
- Dry-vs-cooked is the main trap for an app: dal is about 22-25 g protein per 100 g dry but only about 9 g per 100 g cooked (USDA lentils). A cooked katori of dal is therefore a modest protein source, not a large one; log pulses by dry weight or use cooked values.
- Gap arithmetic (illustration, hypothetical 60 kg man): the national average of about 62 g/day is roughly 1.0 g/kg, versus a 96 g/day floor at 1.6 g/kg, a shortfall of about 34 g/day, and a larger shortfall in quality terms because 40-48% of the average intake is cereal protein.
- Highest-leverage vegetarian additions by protein density (from the table values above): soya chunks (about 52 g/100 g dry), paneer (about 19 g/100 g), firm tofu (about 17 g/100 g), dry pulses (22-25 g/100 g dry). Curd at about 3 g/100 g contributes little unless eaten in large amounts or strained (hung curd/Greek-style).
- Non-vegetarian or ovo-vegetarian users close the gap fastest: chicken breast about 31 g/100 g cooked; eggs about 12.6 g/100 g (USDA).
- Cereal + pulse combinations (dal-rice, dal-roti, khichdi) and adding milk/egg improve amino-acid balance; this follows from the lysine-limitation finding in Swaminathan 2012.
- Paneer and full-fat dairy are also energy-dense, which helps a hardgainer hit a surplus (see section 7), but South Asian cardiometabolic risk (section 8) argues for not relying on saturated-fat-heavy foods alone.

### Gaps
- I could not access the IFCT 2017 PDF or USDA FoodData Central records directly; all food values above are second-hand and should be re-checked. No verified value was obtained for milk, Greek yogurt/hung curd, whey protein powder (per scoop), peanuts, chana (roasted), besan, rajma or fish; do not print numbers for these without looking them up.
- Per-serving conversions (katori, slice, cup) were not sourced; serving sizes must be defined by the app and computed from per-100 g values.
- No data found on protein intake specifically among South Asian men who lift, or among diaspora (UK/US/Canada) South Asians.
- The MoSPI press note itself could not be opened; figures are from press coverage.

## 4. Carbohydrate and fat; meal timing and pre/post-workout nutrition

### Takeaway
After calories and protein are set, fat should be moderate and the remaining calories should come mostly from carbohydrate; precise timing matters little. Total daily protein and evenly spaced protein feedings are the priority; the "anabolic window" is wide (hours, not minutes).

### Cited Findings
- Iraki et al. 2019: fat intake should generally not fall below 0.5 g/kg/day in the off-season. **[secondary for this specific figure]** — [HSN summary of Iraki 2019](https://www.hsnstore.eu/blog/?p=73868); paper: [Iraki et al. 2019](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/7/7/154)
- Iraki et al. 2019: protein should be distributed evenly across 3-6 meals. **[abstract]** — [Iraki et al. 2019](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/7/7/154)
- ISSN position stand on nutrient timing (Kerksick et al. 2017, JISSN 14:33): "Meeting the total daily intake of protein, preferably with evenly spaced protein feedings (approximately every 3 h during the day), should be viewed as a primary area of emphasis for exercising individuals." Post-exercise ingestion (immediately to 2 h post) of high-quality protein stimulates robust increases in muscle protein synthesis, and the size and timing of a pre-exercise meal affects how much post-exercise protein feeding is needed. **[abstract]** — [Kerksick et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0189-4)
- Schoenfeld, Aragon & Krieger 2013 protein-timing meta-analysis, as described by its lead author: there "does not appear to be a narrow 'anabolic window of opportunity'"; any window is wide (about 4-6 hours) and depends on when the pre-workout meal was eaten. **[secondary: author's own blog; 2013, older than the preferred window]** — [Schoenfeld blog](https://www.lookgreatnaked.com/blog/our-meta-analysis-of-protein-timing-thoughts-and-perspectives/)
- ISSN protein stand: 30-40 g casein before sleep increases overnight muscle protein synthesis and metabolic rate without affecting lipolysis. **[abstract]** — [Jäger et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)

### Inferences
- Priority order for the app's coaching rules: (1) calorie surplus, (2) daily protein, (3) protein spread across 3-5 meals, (4) everything else. Timing rules should be framed as convenience, not necessity.
- Practical timing rule consistent with the evidence: have a protein-containing meal within a few hours either side of training; if training fasted (e.g. early morning), eat protein soon after.
- For a hardgainer, carbohydrate is the easiest lever for adding calories (rice, roti, potatoes, fruit, milk), and a traditional South Asian diet is already carbohydrate-rich, so the usual bottleneck is protein and total calories, not carbohydrate.
- A pre-bed dairy protein feeding (milk, curd, paneer, or casein) is a low-effort way to add both protein and calories.

### Gaps
- Iraki 2019's full macronutrient ranges (as I recall the abstract: fat 0.5-1.5 g/kg/day; carbohydrate ≥3-5 g/kg/day; protein within 1-2 hours pre- and post-training) were not surfaced by search in this session apart from the 0.5 g/kg fat floor; verify in the paper before quoting.
- No hypertrophy-specific trial comparing carbohydrate or fat levels at matched calories and protein was retrieved.
- No evidence retrieved on meal frequency per se (independent of protein distribution) and muscle gain.
- No South Asian-specific guidance found on carbohydrate quality during a bulk for people at elevated diabetes risk; this needs clinical input for anyone with prediabetes/diabetes.

## 5. Supplements: what has good evidence and what does not

### Takeaway
Creatine monohydrate (3-5 g/day) and protein powder (as a convenient way to hit the protein target) are the only muscle-gain supplements with strong support; caffeine (3-6 mg/kg) helps performance acutely. Creatine adds roughly 1 kg of lean mass on average over training alone, with part of the early gain likely water, and has not been shown to harm kidney function in healthy people. Vitamin D and B12 are worth testing/correcting in South Asian vegetarians for health reasons, not as muscle builders. BCAAs, glutamine and "testosterone boosters" lack support.

### Cited Findings
**Creatine: effect size**
- Systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis comparing novice and experienced lifters (JISSN, published December 2025; 61 trials): creatine plus resistance training increased fat-free mass by WMD 1.39 kg (95% CI 1.07 to 1.70) vs placebo; both novice and experienced lifters gained. **[abstract]** — [JISSN 2025, PMC12777911](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12777911/)
- Desai et al. 2024 (J Strength Cond Res) meta-analysis of 12 trials in adults under 50: creatine plus resistance training increased lean body mass by 1.14 kg (95% CI 0.69 to 1.59) and reduced body-fat percentage by 0.88 points (95% CI -1.66 to -0.11); no differences by training status. **[secondary summary]** — [Mountain Tactical research review](https://mtntactical.com/knowledge/research-review-creatine-supplementation-increases-muscle-growth-and-reduces-fat-during-resistance-training/)
- Pashayee-Khamene et al. 2024 (JISSN) meta-analysis of 143 studies: weighted mean difference of 0.82 kg in fat-free mass; effects more robust when combined with resistance training. **[secondary summary of abstract]** — [JISSN Dec 2024 record](https://doaj.org/article/18b7ef6d26854885a6a44354f26e3240)
- Contrary single trial: Desai et al. 2025 (Nutrients 17(6):1081), 54 participants, 5 g/day creatine with a one-week wash-in and no loading, 12 weeks resistance training: both creatine and placebo groups gained about 2 kg lean body mass with no significant difference; the creatine group gained about 0.5 kg more lean mass in the first (pre-training) week, mainly in women, which the authors attribute possibly to fluid; they suggest a higher dose may be needed. **[university press release]** — [UNSW newsroom](https://www.unsw.edu.au/newsroom/news/2025/03/sports-supplement-creatine-makes-no-difference-to-muscle-gains-trial-finds)

**Creatine: dose and safety**
- Recommended dose 3-5 g/day or 0.1 g/kg body mass/day, described as relatively well tolerated (Antonio et al. 2021, JISSN 18:13). Water retention is the most common effect and occurs in the first several days; the hair-loss concern rests on one unreplicated study of DHT in rugby players, and no study has reported hair loss. **[secondary summary of paper]** — [Antonio et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7871530); [NutraIngredients](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2021/02/16/Roundup-by-experts-puts-to-rest-common-creatine-misconceptions)
- Iraki et al. 2019 list creatine monohydrate at 3-5 g/day. **[abstract]** — [Iraki et al. 2019](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/7/7/154)
- ISSN position stand (Kreider et al. 2017, JISSN 14:18): short- and long-term supplementation "(up to 30 g/day for 5 years) is safe and well-tolerated in healthy individuals" and a number of patient populations; no compelling evidence that creatine negatively affects renal function in healthy or clinical populations. **[abstract mirror]** — [Kreider et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/5469049/)
- Kidney function, 2025 meta-analysis (BMC Nephrology; studies Jan 2000-Mar 2025; 12 studies with creatinine data): small statistically significant rise in serum creatinine (MD 0.07 µmol/L as reported; 95% CI 0.01 to 0.12; p=0.03), concentrated in trials of ≤1 week, with no significant change in GFR; authors attribute the creatinine rise to creatine metabolism rather than renal impairment. Most participants were young and healthy; GFR evidence is thinner than creatinine evidence. **[abstract]** — [BMC Nephrology 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41199218/)
- Kidney function, 2019 meta-analysis (J Renal Nutr; 15 studies qualitative, 6 quantitative): creatine did not significantly alter serum creatinine. **[abstract]** — [de Souza e Silva et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31375416/). The 2019 and 2025 meta-analyses disagree on whether creatinine rises; neither found reduced kidney function.
- Because creatine raises blood creatinine independently of kidney damage, kidney assessment in creatine users should not rely on serum creatinine alone (2026 narrative review, per search summary); people with existing kidney conditions are advised to avoid creatine. **[secondary]** — [Antonio et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7871530); [Optimum Nutrition summary, manufacturer source](https://www.optimumnutrition.com/en-gb/advice/nutrition/creatine-separating-fact-from-fiction)

**Protein powder**
- Protein supplementation adds +0.30 kg fat-free mass and +2.49 kg 1-RM over training alone, with no further benefit once total intake exceeds about 1.6 g/kg/day (Morton 2018). **[abstract]** — [Morton et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)
- A 2019 European Journal of Nutrition review of supplements for muscle mass and strength rated protein and creatine at evidence level A. **[abstract/trade press]** — [Valenzuela et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30604177/); [NutraIngredients](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2019/01/14/Supplements-vary-in-their-effects-on-muscle-mass-and-strength-Review)
- Product-quality caveat specific to India: an expert commenting on the ICMR-NIN 2024 guidance said the caution should be read as avoiding poorly regulated products and that supplements with credible independent third-party certification are safe. **[secondary; expert opinion]** — [The South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/health/icmr-releases-dietary-guidelines-for-indians-after-13-years-says-no-to-protein-supplements/)

**Caffeine**
- ISSN position stand (2021, JISSN 18:1): caffeine consistently improves exercise performance at 3-6 mg/kg body mass, most commonly taken 60 min pre-exercise; benefits include muscular endurance, movement velocity and muscular strength; doses ≥9 mg/kg have a high incidence of side effects and no additional benefit. **[abstract]** — [Guest et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7777221/)
- Iraki et al. 2019 list caffeine 5-6 mg/kg, beta-alanine 3-5 g/day and citrulline malate 8 g/day as supplements that "might yield ergogenic effects". **[abstract]** — [Iraki et al. 2019](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/7/7/154)

**Vitamin D and B12**
- Vitamin D and muscle: Bislev et al. 2021 (J Bone Miner Res) pooled 54 RCTs, 8,747 participants; no between-group benefit on muscle health measures, and a small negative effect on knee flexion strength (MD -3.3 N, 95% CI -6.63 to -0.03; 12 studies), no longer significant when bolus or >2,800 IU/day studies were excluded. Authors: evidence "does not support a beneficial effect of vitamin D supplementation on muscle health". **[abstract]** — [Bislev et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34405916/). Contradicted in part by Beaudart et al. 2014 (J Clin Endocrinol Metab), which found a small positive effect on global muscle strength (SMD 0.17, P=0.02) but none on muscle mass (SMD 0.058, P=0.52) or power — [Beaudart et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25033068/) (older than the preferred 2018+ window).
- Vitamin D deficiency in India is reported as very common but estimates are low quality: 81.28% of samples deficient in a diagnostic-lab dataset (a tested population, not a random sample). **[secondary; biased sample]** — [Metropolis Healthcare](https://www.metropolisindia.com/blog/nutrition/7-out-of-every-10-indians-are-vitamin-deficient-metropolis-study)
- Vitamin B12: in a community study of 630 urban South Indian adults, deficiency was 35% overall, 54% in vegetarians vs 31% in those on a mixed diet. **[abstract]** — [Status of Vitamin B12 and Folate among the Urban Adult Population in South India](https://doi.org/10.1159/000442677)
- A review of Indian studies reported B12 deficiency prevalence of 78.5% in adults and 61.7% in the elderly using varying cut-offs, and noted that heterogeneous methods and cut-offs prevent firm conclusions. **[abstract]** — [Status of Vitamin B12 among Healthy Adult and Elderly Population in India: A Review](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_2560876623)

**Lacking evidence**
- BCAAs: rated evidence level B (insufficient evidence of benefit on muscle mass or strength) in the 2019 Eur J Nutr review; a 2017 JISSN review by Wolfe argued BCAAs alone do not stimulate muscle growth. A 2024 Nutrition & Metabolism review is more positive, so the literature is mixed. **[abstract/trade press]** — [Valenzuela et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30604177/); [NutraIngredients on Wolfe 2017](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2017/08/24/Limited-evidence-backs-BCAA-s-muscle-building-benefits-says-new-study); [Nutrition & Metabolism 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11220999/)
- Glutamine and Tribulus terrestris: placed in the weakest evidence category ("weak or scarce" evidence for muscle mass or strength) in the 2019 Eur J Nutr review, alongside conjugated linoleic acid, resveratrol and ursolic acid; adverse events have been reported for tribulus at large intakes. **[abstract/trade press]** — [Valenzuela et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30604177/); [NutraIngredients](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2019/01/14/Supplements-vary-in-their-effects-on-muscle-mass-and-strength-Review)

### Inferences
- App supplement tiers: Tier 1 (recommend): creatine monohydrate 3-5 g/day, every day, timing irrelevant; protein powder only if food falls short of the protein target. Tier 2 (optional): caffeine 3-6 mg/kg about 60 min pre-workout (illustration for a hypothetical 60 kg user: 180-360 mg; start low, avoid late-day use because of the sleep findings in section 6). Tier 3 (test, then treat if low): vitamin D and B12, framed as health maintenance. Tier 4 (do not recommend): BCAAs when protein is adequate, glutamine, tribulus/"testosterone boosters".
- Set expectations for creatine at about 1 kg extra lean mass on average over months (range across meta-analyses 0.8-1.4 kg), with an early scale jump that is partly water; the Desai 2025 RCT shows individual trials can find no added benefit at 5 g/day over 12 weeks.
- Vegetarians plausibly respond at least as well to creatine because dietary creatine comes from meat and fish; this is a physiological inference, and I retrieved no trial to cite for it.
- Doctor flags for the app: known kidney disease or reduced kidney function (creatine and high protein); routine blood tests while on creatine (tell the doctor, since creatinine rises without kidney damage); diabetes or hypertension (kidney risk factors); B12 or vitamin D dosing should follow a blood test.
- For Indian users, add a product-quality note: choose third-party-tested protein and creatine.

### Gaps
- The ISSN creatine loading protocol (commonly quoted as about 0.3 g/kg/day for 5-7 days, then 3-5 g/day) was not confirmed in the records accessible this session; loading is optional in any case since 3-5 g/day reaches saturation more slowly. Verify in Kreider et al. 2017.
- The serum-creatinine mean difference in the 2025 BMC Nephrology meta-analysis is reported here as "0.07 µmol/L" exactly as surfaced; the unit looks implausibly small and should be checked against the paper.
- The Desai 2024 meta-analysis numbers come from a secondary summary; I could not confirm which PubMed record is the paper.
- No RCT found on creatine in South Asian or vegetarian lifters specifically.
- No high-quality population-representative prevalence figure found for vitamin D deficiency in South Asian adults; available numbers are lab-convenience samples or clinician opinion.
- No evidence retrieved on whether correcting vitamin D or B12 deficiency improves hypertrophy in deficient lifters specifically.
- Not researched: beta-alanine and citrulline effect sizes, HMB, ashwagandha, mass-gainer powders, omega-3.

## 6. Sleep, recovery and alcohol

### Takeaway
Short sleep measurably blunts muscle protein synthesis and worsens the hormonal environment within days, and heavy drinking after training suppresses muscle protein synthesis even when protein is eaten. Seven or more hours of sleep per night is the consensus adult recommendation.

### Cited Findings
- Lamon et al. 2021 (Physiological Reports 9(1):e14660): randomised crossover, 13 healthy young adults (7 men, 6 women); one night of total sleep deprivation reduced postprandial muscle protein synthesis by 18% (0.072±0.015 vs 0.059±0.014 %/h, p=0.040), raised plasma cortisol 21% (p=0.030) and lowered plasma testosterone 24% (p=0.029); no difference in markers of protein degradation. Authors describe this as anabolic resistance. **[abstract]** — [Lamon et al. 2021](https://www.pubmed.gov/33400856/)
- Saner et al. 2020 (J Physiol): 24 healthy young men; five nights of 4 h time in bed lowered myofibrillar protein synthesis (1.24 %/day) vs normal sleep (1.53 %/day); a sleep-restricted group that also did high-intensity interval exercise maintained rates (1.61 %/day), P<0.05. **[abstract]** — [Saner et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7217042)
- AASM/Sleep Research Society consensus (Watson et al. 2015): adults aged 18-60 should sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis; 6 or fewer hours is inadequate to sustain health; more than 9 hours may be appropriate for young adults, people recovering from sleep debt and people with illness. **[consensus statement]** — [AASM/SRS consensus PDF](https://www.aasm.org/resources/pdf/adultsleepdurationconsensus.pdf); [AASM announcement](https://aasm.org/aasm-and-srs-publish-new-sleep-duration-consensus-statement/)
- Nedeltcheva et al. 2010: experimental sleep restriction during a 14-day diet-induced weight-loss study promoted retention of fat mass and loss of fat-free mass. **[secondary: described in a later review; 2010, older than preferred window]** — [Review citing Nedeltcheva 2010, PMC11266528](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11266528/)
- Alcohol: Parr et al. 2014 (PLoS One 9(2):e88384): 8 physically active men, crossover, resistance plus cycling exercise; versus protein alone, post-exercise myofibrillar protein synthesis was 24% lower with alcohol plus protein and 37% lower with alcohol plus carbohydrate (protein synthesis still rose above rest in all conditions). Authors conclude alcohol suppresses the anabolic response and may impair recovery and adaptation. **[abstract]** — [Parr et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24533082/)

### Inferences
- App rule: target ≥7 h sleep per night; treat regularly sleeping ≤6 h as a recovery limiter worth flagging before adding training volume or supplements.
- Saner 2020 implies training during a short-sleep stretch is still worthwhile (exercise preserved protein synthesis), so the coaching message on a bad-sleep day is "train anyway, maybe lighter", not "skip".
- Alcohol rule: avoid heavy drinking in the hours after training; if drinking, still eat protein (the protein condition lost less: 24% vs 37%). This is acute-mechanism evidence, not long-term muscle-gain evidence.
- Late caffeine can undermine the sleep target, so pre-workout caffeine should be restricted for evening trainers (inference from combining sections 5 and 6; no caffeine-sleep trial was retrieved).

### Gaps
- All sleep findings above are acute protein-synthesis studies (1-5 nights). I found no long-term RCT in this session showing that habitual short sleep reduces hypertrophy from resistance training, or that sleep extension increases it.
- Nedeltcheva 2010's specific figures (commonly quoted as 5.5 vs 8.5 h in bed, with a markedly lower fat share of weight lost and more fat-free mass lost) could not be verified; not reported.
- Parr 2014's alcohol dose (as I recall, about 1.5 g/kg, a binge-level intake) was not confirmed in the accessible record; verify before stating a dose. No evidence was retrieved on moderate drinking (1-2 drinks) and muscle gain.
- No evidence retrieved on rest days, stress, or deloads; these belong to the training-programming notes.

## 7. Why some people struggle to gain weight, and tactics for eating more

### Takeaway
"Hardgainers" are real in the sense that people differ about tenfold in fat gain from the same overfeeding, largely because some unconsciously burn off extra calories through non-exercise activity (NEAT); many also simply under-eat when left to appetite. Tactics with support are a structured eating plan rather than eating to appetite, and liquid calories, which the body compensates for poorly.

### Cited Findings
- Levine, Eberhardt & Jensen 1999 (Science 283:212-214): during overfeeding, two-thirds of the rise in total daily energy expenditure came from increased non-exercise activity thermogenesis (fidgeting, posture, daily movement); the authors conclude that activation of NEAT dissipates excess energy to preserve leanness and failure to activate it results in ready fat gain. **[abstract; 1999, older than preferred window but foundational]** — [Levine et al. 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9880251/)
- Levine 1999 design and spread, per secondary summaries: 16 adults overfed 1,000 kcal/day for 8 weeks; fat gain ranged from 0.36 kg to 4.23 kg (about tenfold); change in NEAT ranged from -98 to +692 kcal/day and predicted resistance to fat gain (r=0.77, p<0.001). **[secondary]** — [Bodyrecomposition.com review](https://bodyrecomposition.com/?p=4170)
- DiMeglio & Mattes 2000 (Int J Obes): crossover, 7 men and 8 women given 1,880 kJ/day of carbohydrate as liquid (soda) or solid (jelly beans) for 4 weeks each. Free-living intake fell to compensate during the solid period but did not decrease during the liquid period; body weight and BMI increased significantly only during the liquid period. Authors: liquid carbohydrate "promotes positive energy balance", whereas solid carbohydrate elicits precise dietary compensation. **[abstract; 2000, older than preferred window]** — [DiMeglio & Mattes 2000](https://www.nature.com/articles/0801229)
- Later reviews: dietary compensation for beverages is weaker than for solid foods of comparable nutrient content; soup is an exception (increases satiety). **[review]** — [PMC2664987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2664987)
- Energy density: higher energy-density diets lead to higher energy intake and body weight, lower energy-density diets reduce them (summary of Rolls's work). **[secondary]** — [PMC4597097](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4597097)
- Garthe et al. 2013: athletes given a counselled meal plan with a planned energy surplus gained 3.9% body weight vs 1.5% in those eating ad libitum over the same strength-training period. **[abstract]** — [Garthe et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23679146/)
- Slater et al. 2019 explicitly address "the management of satiety when attempting to increase energy intake" as a practical barrier. **[abstract]** — [Slater et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/full)
- The vegan group in Monteyne 2023 ate roughly double the fibre, and it was anecdotally harder for those participants to eat the required protein. **[secondary]** — [Monteyne et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36822394/)

### Inferences
- Core coaching rule: do not rely on appetite. Give the user a calorie number and a meal plan, and use the weekly weight trend as the feedback loop (Garthe 2013: planned eating produced 2.6 times the weight gain of ad libitum eating). If the scale has not moved in 2 weeks, the user is not in a surplus regardless of how much they feel they eat, and NEAT compensation (Levine 1999) explains why an on-paper surplus can vanish.
- Tactic list consistent with the evidence: (a) drink some calories: milk, lassi, a milk + banana + peanut butter + protein shake (weak compensation for liquids, DiMeglio & Mattes 2000); (b) raise energy density: nuts, peanut butter, ghee/oil in moderation, paneer, dried fruit, whole milk instead of low-volume/high-fibre options; (c) add an extra meal or pre-bed snack rather than bigger plates; (d) do not start meals with soup, salad or large volumes of water, which increase fullness.
- For vegetarians, high-fibre pulses and vegetables fill the stomach before the calorie/protein target is reached (Monteyne 2023 anecdote); soy chunks, paneer, tofu, curd and protein powder deliver protein with less bulk.
- Liquid sugar is the caveat: the DiMeglio study used soda. For a South Asian man with elevated diabetes risk (section 8), liquid calories should be milk/protein/fat/fruit based, not sugar-sweetened drinks. This is my inference, not something a retrieved trial tested.

### Gaps
- Slater 2019's specific recommendations on satiety (energy-dense foods, liquids, meal frequency) could not be read because the full text was inaccessible; the tactic list above is inference from the other cited studies.
- No trial found testing appetite or eating tactics in lean men trying to gain weight for muscle; liquid-calorie and energy-density evidence comes from obesity research applied in reverse.
- Levine 1999 participant numbers and ranges come from secondary summaries only.
- No evidence retrieved on genetics of low appetite or gastric emptying in constitutionally lean people. Unexplained inability to gain weight, or unintentional weight loss, should be referred to a doctor (thyroid, malabsorption, diabetes); this is a standard clinical caution with no retrieved citation.

## 8. Body recomposition: when is gaining muscle while losing fat realistic, and how should targets differ?

### Takeaway
Gaining muscle without a surplus is realistic mainly for novices and people with higher body fat; a deficit of about 500 kcal/day on average cancels lean-mass gains. A "skinny-fat" South Asian man (normal BMI, high body fat, low lean mass, a common phenotype) should train hard at maintenance or a very small surplus with high protein, while a genuinely lean, underweight man should run a surplus.

### Cited Findings
- Murphy & Koehler 2022 (Scand J Med Sci Sports 32(1)): meta-analysis and meta-regression of RCTs of resistance training in an energy deficit for ≥3 weeks. Lean-mass gains were impaired in a deficit vs energy balance (effect size -0.57, p=0.02); strength gains were comparable (ES -0.31, p=0.28); the meta-regression found that an energy deficit of about 500 kcal/day prevented gains in lean mass. Evidence in experienced lifters is limited. **[abstract]** — [Murphy & Koehler 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14075)
- Barakat et al. 2020 (Strength Cond J 42(5)), "Body Recomposition: Can Trained Individuals Build Muscle and Lose Fat at the Same Time?": narrative review arguing the literature supports recomposition in trained individuals too, not only novices or people with obesity, conditional on resistance training and high protein intake. **[secondary summary]** — [mysportscience summary](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/can-i-gain-muscle-and-lose-weight-at-the-same-time)
- Helms et al. 2023: across maintenance, 5% and 15% surplus groups there was no evidence of group differences in muscle-thickness gain over 8 weeks, i.e. the maintenance group was not shown to grow less. **[abstract]** — [Helms et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37914977/)
- ISSN protein stand: higher protein, 2.3-3.1 g/kg/day, may be needed to retain lean mass in resistance-trained people during hypocaloric periods. **[abstract]** — [Jäger et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)
- South Asian body composition: at similar or lower BMI, South Asians have lower lean mass and higher body-fat percentage than populations of European ancestry; South Asian men have about 4-7 percentage points higher body fat at the same BMI and about 3.0-3.6 kg less lean body mass than Chinese, Aboriginal or European men; the high-fat/low-lean phenotype is linked to higher insulin and HOMA values. **[review]** — [Review, PMC8617748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8617748/)
- "Thin-fat" phenotype: normal body weight by BMI but disproportionately high body fat; classically described in the 2004 Lancet "Y-Y paradox" paper. One review lists body-fat cut-offs of 20% for South Asian men vs 25% for Western men. **[review]** — [Endotext, Thin Fat Obesity](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK568563/)
- WHO expert consultation 2004 (Lancet 363:157-163): a substantial share of Asian people face high risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at BMIs below 25 kg/m²; additional public-health action points were identified at 23.0, 27.5, 32.5 and 37.5 kg/m²; no single cut-off was set for all Asian populations. **[abstract]** — [WHO expert consultation 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14726171)

### Inferences
- Branching logic for the app's worked example (38-year-old South Asian man who "looks skinny"):
  - Lean and underweight/low-normal BMI with a small waist: lean bulk. Surplus about 10-20%, gain 0.25-0.5% of body weight per week, protein 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day (section 1-2).
  - "Skinny-fat" (thin limbs, soft midsection, normal BMI, higher waist): recomposition. Eat at about maintenance (or a very small surplus), protein at the upper end (about 2.0-2.2 g/kg/day), progressive resistance training, track waist and strength rather than scale weight. As a novice he can expect to gain muscle without a surplus (Barakat 2020; Helms 2023 maintenance arm).
  - Never combine "wants to get bigger" with a deficit of 500 kcal/day or more; that level on average eliminates lean-mass gain (Murphy & Koehler 2022).
- BMI alone will misclassify South Asian men: "skinny" by appearance can coexist with high body fat. The app should ask for waist circumference (or waist-to-height) in addition to weight, and treat BMI ≥23 as the point to stop a bulk and reassess rather than BMI 25 (WHO 2004 action point).
- Because South Asians carry higher diabetes risk at lower BMI, the bulk should be deliberately conservative (top out at the 0.5%/week band, prefer protein- and whole-food-based calories over sugar), and the app should suggest a baseline health check (fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipids) for a 38-year-old South Asian man before a prolonged surplus. This is a risk-based inference; no retrieved trial tested bulking in this group.
- Anyone with diabetes or prediabetes needs individual medical/dietetic advice before a calorie surplus or high-carbohydrate plan.

### Gaps
- Barakat 2020 was not accessible; its specific protein recommendation and study list are unverified (one blog quotes 1.6-2.4 g/kg/day; not confirmed).
- No RCT found comparing maintenance vs surplus specifically in untrained "skinny-fat" men, nor any recomposition trial in South Asians.
- Rates of muscle gain realistically achievable by novice vs intermediate natural lifters (kg per month) were not sourced in this session; the commonly repeated rules of thumb are not from controlled trials and are omitted.
- South Asian waist-circumference cut-offs (commonly cited as 90 cm for men) were not retrieved from a primary source in this session; verify before using.
- The WHO 2004 full text was not read; action-point wording is from the abstract.
