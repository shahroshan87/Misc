# South Asian considerations: body composition, cardiometabolic health and response to training (for a lean South Asian man in his late 30s who wants to gain muscle)

> Research method note (read first). Compiled 2026-11-17. Full-text fetching of PubMed/PMC was blocked in this session (host not on the network allowlist) and the web-search budget ran out before the last four queries. Every figure below was taken from search-result extracts of the cited abstract, paper or guideline page, not from a full-text read, unless stated. Items I could not verify this session are listed under "Gaps" (some with the value I recall, explicitly labelled as unverified recall). All statistics are population averages from specific cohorts; none of them is a fact about an individual user.

## 1. Body composition: is there a "thin-fat" phenotype (less lean mass, more total/visceral/ectopic fat at a given BMI)?

### Takeaway
Well established at population level: at the same BMI, South Asian adults on average carry more body fat and less lean mass than white Europeans, with a tendency to more abdominal/ectopic (liver, intermuscular) fat. The size of the lean-mass gap in men is roughly 3-3.5 kg after adjustment in the best-known multi-ethnic study; whether visceral fat specifically is higher is less consistent across cohorts than the total-fat and lean-mass findings.

### Cited Findings
- Lean mass: in the Canadian M-CHAT study (828 adults: 196 Aboriginal, 222 Chinese, 202 European, 208 South Asian, recruited across the BMI range), after adjustment South Asian men had 3.57 kg less lean mass than European men, 3.42 kg (95% CI 1.55-5.29) less than Aboriginal men and 3.01 kg less than Chinese men; South Asian women had 2.97 kg less than European women. Insulin and HOMA were highest in South Asians, but the differences were no longer apparent once the fat-to-lean-mass ratio was accounted for. Authors: "South Asians have a phenotype of high fat mass and low lean mass" (Lear et al., J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2009;94:4696-702; cross-sectional) — [EurekAlert release of the paper](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/725895); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/10/091006093343.htm)
- Body fat at a given BMI: in New Zealand adults, Asian Indian men with a BMI of about 24 kg/m² (women: 26) had the same percentage body fat as Europeans with a BMI of 30 kg/m² (Rush, Freitas & Plank, Br J Nutr 2009;102:632-41). An accompanying commentary describes Asian Indians as showing the greatest deviation from whites, "up to 5 % higher body fat at any BMI value" — [Rush et al. 2009, Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/87A0A5850DC9BA61F69EA944E26EA1E3/core-reader)
- The "Y-Y paradox" illustration: the two authors had the same BMI (22.3 kg/m²) but body fat of 9.1% (Yudkin, British) vs 21.2% (Yajnik, Indian) (Yajnik & Yudkin, Lancet 2004;363:163). This is an n=2 illustration, not a study — [The Economist summary](https://econ.st/3TJAm4Q); context in [Endotext "Thin-fat obesity"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK568563/)
- MASALA vs MESA (US, ages ~40-84): compared with all four MESA ethnic groups (White, Chinese American, African American, Latino), South Asians had greater intermuscular fat, lower hepatic attenuation (more liver fat) and less lean mass; authors conclude a "less favorable body composition and adipokine profile" (Shah et al., Int J Obes 2016;40:639-45) — [Shah et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2015.219). Note: a related MASALA-MESA analysis reported greater hepatic steatosis but comparable visceral fat area in South Asians, so "more visceral fat" is not uniform across cohorts (per search summary of the MASALA literature; the exact companion paper was not retrieved).
- Visceral fat: in M-CHAT, percentage body fat was higher for a given BMI in South Asians, and visceral adipose tissue (VAT) was higher for a given waist circumference in both Chinese and South Asian men and women (Lear et al., Obesity 2007) — [Lear et al. 2007, Obesity](https://doi.org/10.1038/oby.2007.334). A companion paper concluded that Chinese and South Asian groups had relatively more abdominal fat than Europeans, "more pronounced with VAT" (Lear et al., Am J Clin Nutr 2007;86:353-9) — [AJCN 2007 (DOI from memory, verify)](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/86.2.353)
- Fat-cell morphology in young lean men: in GlasVEGAS (14 South Asian, 21 white European men, early 20s, BMI ~22 kg/m²), South Asian men at baseline had fewer small adipocytes (37.1% vs 60.0%, P = 0.003) and more large adipocytes (26.2% vs 9.1%, P = 0.005), and more whole-body and abdominal subcutaneous fat, than white Europeans (McLaren et al., Nat Metab 2024) — [GlasVEGAS full paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42255-024-01101-z); [PMC copy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11349579/)
- Metabolic abnormality at normal weight: in pooled MASALA/MESA data (normal-weight participants: 846 White, 323 Chinese American, 334 African American, 252 Hispanic, 195 South Asian), South Asian Americans at normal weight were about twice as likely as whites to have two or more cardiometabolic abnormalities (high BP, elevated glucose, low HDL, high triglycerides); not explained by demographics, health behaviours or ectopic fat. Normal weight was defined as BMI 18.5-22.9 for South Asians (Gujral et al., Ann Intern Med 2017) — [UCSF release](https://www.ucsf.edu/news/2017/04/406316/race-ranks-higher-pounds-diabetes-heart-health-risks)
- Counterpoint: a Singapore study found insulin resistance in South Asians is less affected by obesity and fat distribution than in Chinese, and that lean South Asians were also insulin resistant, i.e. the fat-vs-lean explanation does not account for everything — [A*STAR research highlight](https://research.a-star.edu.sg/articles/highlights/investigating-asian-ethnic-differences/)

### Inferences
- ESTABLISHED (population level): higher % body fat and lower lean mass at a given BMI; replicated in Canada, NZ, the US and the UK with different methods (DXA, CT, MRI).
- LESS CONSISTENT: "more visceral fat" specifically. Liver fat, intermuscular fat and large subcutaneous adipocytes are at least as consistently reported as VAT.
- For a "skinny" South Asian man, a normal or low BMI does not reliably indicate low body fat or good metabolic health; a waist measure adds information BMI lacks. This is a reason to measure, not to assume the individual is "thin-fat".
- Building muscle moves the fat-to-lean ratio in the direction that statistically explained the ethnic difference in insulin/HOMA in Lear 2009. That is a plausible mechanism-based argument for resistance training in this group, but it rests on a cross-sectional association, not on trial evidence of disease prevention.

### Gaps
- UK Biobank imaging/DXA estimates of the lean-mass difference in South Asian vs white men (the search for this was not executed; budget exhausted). I found no UK Biobank figure to cite.
- Almost all comparative body-composition cohorts are middle-aged or older (MASALA 40-84; M-CHAT adults across ages); data specific to men aged 30-45 are thin. GlasVEGAS (early 20s) is the main young-male dataset and is small (n=35).
- Most data are on people of Indian origin or pooled "South Asian"; separate data for Pakistani, Bangladeshi, Sri Lankan and Nepali men are sparse.
- Exact visceral-fat magnitude (cm² or litres at matched BMI) was not retrieved.

## 2. BMI and waist cut-offs for South Asians

### Takeaway
All major bodies use lower BMI thresholds for South Asians: 23 kg/m² for overweight/increased risk and either 25 (Indian consensus) or 27.5 (NICE/WHO action point) for obesity; waist ≥90 cm in men (≥80 cm women) is the Indian/Asian abdominal-obesity cut-off, and NICE adds "keep waist under half your height". Risk-equivalence studies in UK data put the South Asian BMI equivalent of a white BMI of 30 at about 22-24 kg/m².

### Cited Findings
- NICE NG246 (overweight and obesity management; replaced CG189/PH46): for people with a South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean family background, overweight is BMI 23-27.4 kg/m² and obesity is BMI ≥27.5 kg/m², because these groups are prone to central adiposity and cardiometabolic risk occurs at lower BMI. Measure waist-to-height ratio in people with BMI <35; advice is to "try and keep their waist to less than half their height" (ratio <0.5) — [NICE NG246, identification chapter](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG246/chapter/Identifying-and-assessing-overweight-obesity-and-central-adiposity)
- Secondary summaries of NICE give waist-to-height bands of 0.5-0.59 = increased central adiposity and ≥0.6 = high, and note obesity classes 2 and 3 thresholds are usually lowered by 2.5 kg/m² for these groups (not confirmed on the NICE page in this session) — [Diabetes on the Net NICE factsheet](https://diabetesonthenet.com/wp-content/uploads/51.-Factsheet_NICE-obesity-guidance.pdf); [GPnotebook](https://gpnotebook.com/pages/obesity/waist-to-height-ratio-whr)
- Indian consensus (Misra, Chowbey, Makkar et al., J Assoc Physicians India 2009;57:163-70): overweight BMI 23-24.9 kg/m², obesity BMI ≥25 kg/m²; abdominal obesity waist ≥90 cm in men and ≥80 cm in women (vs European 102/88 cm) — [PubMed 19582986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19582986/)
- A newer Indian revision (2025) criticises the 2009 statement for relying solely on BMI and proposes a definition requiring BMI >23 kg/m² plus excess waist circumference or waist-to-height ratio — [Revised definition of obesity in Asian Indians living in India (PDF)](https://www.cmcendovellore.org/pub/2025/revised-definition-of-obesity-in-asian-indians-living-in-india.pdf)
- Risk-equivalence, England primary care (1,472,819 people, 75,956 South Asian): the BMI giving the same age- and sex-adjusted type 2 diabetes incidence as BMI 30.0 in White people was 23.9 kg/m² (95% CI 23.6-24.0) in South Asians (28.1 Black, 26.9 Chinese, 26.6 Arab) (Caleyachetty et al., Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2021;9:419-26) — [LSHTM repository record](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/4661018)
- Risk-equivalence, UK Biobank (490,288 participants, 9,631 South Asian; cross-sectional): the BMI with the same diabetes prevalence as BMI 30 in white participants was 22.0 kg/m² in South Asians (21.5 in Pakistani men, 22.0 in Indian men per press summary); a waist of 102 cm in white men equated to 79 cm in South Asian men (88 cm white women = 70 cm South Asian women) (Ntuk et al., Diabetes Care 2014;37:2500-7) — [Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/93767); [UK Biobank publication page](https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/publications/ethnic-specific-obesity-cutoffs-for-diabetes-risk-cross-sectional-study-of-490288-uk-biobank-participants/)
- US: for diabetes-screening purposes the ADA defines overweight as BMI ≥25 kg/m², or ≥23 kg/m² in Asian American individuals; the USPSTF also suggests considering BMI ≥23 for Asian American patients — [AAFP summary of USPSTF](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2022/0100/od1); [secondary summary of ADA 2026 Standards](https://www.iatrox.com/clinical-guidance/us/prediabetes-type-2-diabetes-screening-diagnosis)

### Inferences
- For an app: flag BMI ≥23 as the "pay attention" line for South Asian users (consistent across NICE, Indian consensus, ADA), and use waist <90 cm and waist-to-height <0.5 as the practical ceilings for men. For most men the 0.5 ratio is the stricter test (a 170 cm man would cross it at 85 cm).
- The Ntuk waist equivalent (79 cm in men) is a prevalence-equivalence statistic from a 40-69-year-old cohort, not a guideline cut-off; it shows direction and size of the shift, and should not be presented as a target.
- These thresholds were derived for diabetes/cardiovascular risk in general populations. They do not distinguish muscle from fat, so a trained man gaining lean mass can cross BMI 23 without the risk the cut-off is meant to capture; waist measures are the better tripwire during a bulk.

### Gaps
- WHO expert consultation (Lancet 2004;363:157-63) was not retrieved this session. Unverified recall: it kept the international BMI categories but added public-health action points at 23, 27.5, 32.5 and 37.5 kg/m² for Asian populations. Verify against the paper before quoting.
- Unverified recall: Misra et al. 2006 (Int J Obes) proposed waist "action level 1" of 78 cm (men) and 72 cm (women) and "action level 2" of 90/80 cm for Asian Indians; the IDF uses ≥90/≥80 cm for South Asians. Not confirmed this session.
- Exact staging and waist/WHtR numbers of the 2025 Indian revised obesity definition (the search for it was not executed).

## 3. Cardiometabolic risk and recommended screening

### Takeaway
Well established: South Asians develop type 2 diabetes and coronary disease at lower BMI and younger ages (diabetes roughly 5-10 years earlier; first heart attack about 6 years earlier in INTERHEART), largely through higher levels of conventional risk factors at younger ages. Guidelines therefore treat South Asian ancestry as a reason for earlier and fuller risk assessment: glucose/HbA1c from age 25 in the UK (especially at BMI ≥23), South Asian ancestry as a cholesterol "risk-enhancer" in the US, and lipoprotein(a) measured at least once.

### Cited Findings
- Age at first heart attack: INTERHEART (1,732 South Asian cases and 2,204 controls from 15 centres in 5 South Asian countries vs 10,728 cases and 12,431 controls elsewhere): mean age at first acute MI 53.0 (SD 11.4) years in South Asian countries vs 58.8 (SD 12.2) years in other countries (P<.001); explained largely by higher risk-factor levels at younger ages (<60) (Joshi et al., JAMA 2007;297:286-94). Note this compares people living in South Asia with the rest of the world, not migrants with whites in one country — [PubMed 17227980](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17227980/)
- US: the AHA scientific statement (Volgman et al., Circulation 2018;138:e1-e34) reports South Asians in the US have higher ASCVD mortality than East Asians and non-Hispanic whites; most of the excess risk is explained by a higher prevalence of known risk factors, especially those related to insulin resistance; the most significant disparity is excess type 2 diabetes and impaired glucose tolerance; diet high in refined carbohydrate and saturated fat (including in vegetarians) is singled out — [Circulation PDF](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/pdf/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000580); [AHA release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/685174)
- The AHA statement advises calculating ASCVD risk with the pooled cohort equations, while acknowledging they are not validated in South Asians; commentary notes QRISK2 as an alternative and that the 2018 AHA/ACC multi-society cholesterol guideline lists South Asian ancestry as a risk-enhancing factor when deciding on statins — [AHA Professional Heart Daily commentary](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/atherosclerotic-cardiovascular-disease-in-south-asians-in-the-united-states-epidemiology/commentary)
- National Lipid Association (Kalra et al., J Clin Lipidol 2021): South Asian descent is a risk-enhancing factor; test lipids including Lp(a); consider coronary artery calcium scoring; the NLA infographic recommends the QRISK3 calculator (calibrated and validated in South Asians) — [NLA infographic](https://www.lipid.org/wp-content/uploads/NLA_ASCVD_Mgmt_in_South_Asian_Pop_Infographic.pdf); [ACC summary](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2021/08/02/14/16/south-asian-cardiovascular-health)
- Lipoprotein(a): in INTERHEART (6,086 first-MI cases, 6,857 controls, 7 ethnic groups incl. 1,829 South Asians), Lp(a) >50 mg/dL was associated with MI (OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.32-1.67); the population-attributable risk of high Lp(a) ranged from 0% in Africans to 9.5% in South Asians (Paré et al., Circulation 2019). An editorial calls this "some evidence" needing replication (~9-10% vs ~5% in Europeans) — [PubMed 30667276](https://www.pubmed.gov/30667276/)
- A review reports about 25% of South Asians have Lp(a) >50 mg/dL — [Role of Lp(a) in ASCVD in South Asian individuals](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10945898); but the NLA 2024 focused update notes the hazard per unit of elevated Lp(a) was similar for White, South Asian and Black individuals in one large analysis and that measurement rates are low — [NLA 2024 Lp(a) update](https://www.lipid.org/sites/default/files/files/PIIS1933287424000333.pdf)
- Diabetes prevalence, US: MASALA age-adjusted type 2 diabetes prevalence 23% in South Asians vs 6% in White and 18% in African American MESA participants (ages ~40-84) (reported in Gujral & Kanaya's review of MASALA) — [Gujral & Kanaya, Ann N Y Acad Sci 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.14530)
- Diabetes prevalence, India: ICMR-INDIAB-17 (113,043 adults ≥20 y, surveyed 2008-2020): diabetes 11.4% (95% CI 10.2-12.5), prediabetes 15.3% (13.9-16.6), hypertension 35.5%, generalised obesity 28.6%, abdominal obesity 39.5% (37.7-41.4) (Anjana et al., Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2023) — [Manipal research record](https://researcher.manipal.edu/en/publications/metabolic-non-communicable-disease-health-report-of-india-the-icm/)
- Age at onset, UK/high-income settings: South Asians are diagnosed with type 2 diabetes about 5-10 years earlier than white Europeans and at lower BMI — [Glasgow review (eprint PDF)](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/261034/1/261034.pdf). A 2024 genetic study in British Pakistani and Bangladeshi people (Genes & Health) linked higher genetic burden to earlier onset; high-genetic-risk individuals developed diabetes on average 8 years earlier and at lower BMI — [QMUL release](https://qmul.ac.uk/wiph/news/latest-news/items/study-reveals-genetic-drivers-of-early-onset-type-2-diabetes-in-south-asians.html)
- Insulin resistance in non-diabetic men: in 100 South Asian and 100 European men aged 40-70 in Scotland, HOMA-IR was 67% higher (p<0.001) and fasting glucose 3% higher (p<0.018) in South Asians (Ghouri et al., Diabetologia 2013;56:2238-49) — [PubMed 23811809](https://www.pubmed.gov/23811809/)
- Screening, UK (NICE PH38, per secondary summaries): offer a diabetes risk assessment to adults aged 25+ of South Asian, Chinese, African-Caribbean or Black African descent (vs 40+ for everyone); consider a blood test (fasting glucose or HbA1c) for anyone aged ≥25 of South Asian or Chinese descent with BMI ≥23 regardless of risk score; "high risk" = fasting glucose 5.5-6.9 mmol/L or HbA1c 42-47 mmol/mol; reassess at least every 3-5 years depending on risk (summaries differ on interval) — [GPnotebook summary of NICE PH38](https://gpnotebook.com/en-GB/pages/diabetes-and-endocrinology/pre-diabetes)
- Screening, US: per summaries of the ADA 2026 Standards, test adults with overweight/obesity (BMI ≥23 for Asian Americans) plus one or more risk factors at any age, and everyone else from age 35; USPSTF screens ages 35-70 with overweight/obesity and suggests BMI ≥23 and earlier screening for Asian Americans — [secondary ADA 2026 summary](https://www.iatrox.com/clinical-guidance/us/prediabetes-type-2-diabetes-screening-diagnosis); [AAFP/USPSTF](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2022/0100/od1)

### Inferences
- ESTABLISHED: earlier and higher diabetes and CHD risk at population level; guideline-level endorsement of earlier screening.
- A South Asian man in his late 30s already meets the age criterion for diabetes testing under both UK (≥25) and US (≥35) frameworks. A reasonable baseline panel implied by the guidance above: HbA1c or fasting glucose, a full lipid profile, blood pressure, and a one-off Lp(a) (NLA). The app should frame this as "guidelines suggest asking your doctor", not as a diagnosis or a requirement.
- Family history matters more than ethnicity alone for the individual; ethnicity shifts the prior, the blood tests give the answer.
- CONTESTED/WEAKER: whether Lp(a) is disproportionately important in South Asians (one case-control estimate; similar per-unit hazard elsewhere). The one-off test is still supported by lipid societies for all adults.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the AHA 2018 statement's own wording on HbA1c/BMI-23 screening, Lp(a) or coronary calcium; only press and commentary summaries. Treat specific screening thresholds as coming from NICE/ADA/NLA, not from the AHA statement.
- South Asian Health Foundation (UK), British Heart Foundation and Diabetes UK guidance pages were not retrieved (search budget). No SAHF-specific figure is cited here.
- Unverified recall: the ESC 2021 prevention guideline applies ethnicity multipliers to SCORE2 risk (about 1.3 for Indian/Bangladeshi and 1.7 for Pakistani origin); NHS Health Check covers ages 40-74; QRISK3 includes ethnicity. None confirmed this session.
- Kanaya et al. 2014 (Diabetes Care) primary numbers were not retrieved; the 23% vs 6% figures are from a later review by the same group.
- The exact South Asian percentage in Gujral 2017 was not surfaced (only "about twice as likely as whites"; the search extract gave 21.0% as an overall or reference prevalence, which I could not disambiguate).
- Absolute risk for a lean, non-smoking South Asian man aged 35-40 specifically: no source found; cohorts are mostly 40+.

## 4. Implications for "bulking": should South Asians gain weight more slowly or watch waist?

### Takeaway
There is direct but small-scale experimental evidence that young, lean South Asian men tolerate overfeeding worse than white European men: insulin sensitivity fell 38% vs 7% after ~5-7% weight gain (GlasVEGAS, n=35), and a 5-day high-fat overfeed impaired insulin sensitivity only in South Asians (n=24). No guideline body has issued bulking-specific advice for South Asians; "bulk slowly, cap the gain, watch the waist and re-check glucose" is a reasonable inference from these trials plus the cut-off literature, not an established recommendation.

### Cited Findings
- GlasVEGAS (McLaren et al., Nat Metab 2024): 14 South Asian and 21 white European men, early 20s, BMI ~22 kg/m²; 4-6 weeks of overfeeding to induce 5-7% weight gain (about 5 kg). Total adipose gain was similar, but insulin sensitivity fell 38% in South Asian men vs 7% in white European men (P = 0.009), and the rise in postprandial insulin was about 4-fold greater in South Asians. Per the authors' summary, white European men gained more lean tissue (2.4 vs 0.7 l). Authors attribute the difference partly to adipocyte morphology (fewer small fat cells available to expand) and describe white Europeans as having "metabolic buffering capacity". Small study; funded by EFPIA-IMI EMIF — [Nature Metabolism paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42255-024-01101-z); [authors' "Behind the paper"](https://communities.springernature.com/posts/greater-adverse-consequences-of-weight-gain-in-south-asian-compared-with-white-european-men-findings-from-the-glasvegas-study); [University of Glasgow release](https://www.gla.ac.uk/news/archiveofnews/2024/august/headline_1099926_en.html); [News & Views: "Insights into optimal BMI"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42255-024-01088-7)
- Short-term overfeeding (Bakker et al., Diabetes 2014;63:248-58): 12 healthy lean South Asian men and 12 matched white men aged 19-25 (Netherlands; per the Leiden thesis, with a family history of type 2 diabetes); 5 days of a high-fat, high-calorie diet (extra 375 mL cream/day = 1,275 kcal/day, 94% fat) reduced insulin-stimulated glucose disposal on clamp only in the South Asians; no group differences in liver fat or abdominal fat change, or in muscle insulin signalling — [Bakker et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.2337/db13-0696)
- Resistance training did not cancel the difference in fat response: in the Glasgow 12-week training study, changes in body fat were less favourable in South Asian than white European men despite similar muscle gains (see section 5) — [Alkhayl et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35169204/)
- Risk rises at low BMI: the BMI equivalent to a white BMI of 30 for diabetes risk is about 22.0-23.9 kg/m² in South Asians (Ntuk 2014; Caleyachetty 2021; section 2) — [Caleyachetty et al. 2021](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/4661018); [Ntuk et al. 2014](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/93767)
- Guideline tripwires that apply during weight gain: BMI 23 kg/m² (NICE, Indian consensus, ADA), waist ≥90 cm in men (Indian consensus), waist-to-height ≥0.5 (NICE) — [NICE NG246](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG246/chapter/Identifying-and-assessing-overweight-obesity-and-central-adiposity); [Misra et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19582986/)

### Inferences
- PLAUSIBLE INFERENCE (not guideline): favour a small surplus and slow gain over an aggressive "dirty bulk". GlasVEGAS used a deliberately fast gain (~5 kg in 4-6 weeks) with no training; the 38% drop tells us about rapid fat-dominant overfeeding, and cannot be assumed to apply to a slow surplus combined with resistance training and adequate protein. No trial has tested a training-supported lean bulk in South Asian men.
- The GlasVEGAS lean-tissue result (0.7 vs 2.4 l) is from overfeeding without a training stimulus in a tiny sample; it should not be read as "South Asians partition calories worse when lifting". The training study (section 5) found similar muscle gain.
- Practical monitoring set an app could suggest, each tied to a cited threshold: (a) waist at the navel weekly, aiming to stay <0.5 x height and <90 cm; (b) rate of gain kept modest, with a pause or mini-cut if waist rises faster than lifts and limb measures; (c) HbA1c or fasting glucose, lipids and blood pressure before a long bulk and again afterwards (NICE/ADA already support testing at this age); (d) treat BMI 23 as a prompt to check waist, not as a hard stop for a man who is visibly gaining muscle.
- Composition of the surplus plausibly matters: the 5-day study used a 94%-fat surplus, and the AHA statement singles out refined carbohydrate and saturated fat. Whether a protein/complex-carbohydrate surplus is better tolerated in South Asians has not been tested.
- These are group-average responses in 12-14 men per group; individual variation was large and an individual user may respond like either group.

### Gaps
- No expert body (AHA, ADA, NICE, Indian consensus, sports-nutrition societies) was found to give South Asian-specific advice on rate of weight gain or surplus size for muscle gain. Any number (e.g. "0.25% of body weight per week") would be a general sports-nutrition figure, not South Asian evidence.
- GlasVEGAS absolute values (kg of fat and lean gained per group, Matsuda index values, liver/visceral fat changes, and whether changes reversed with the subsequent weight-loss phase) were not retrieved; the "2.4 vs 0.7 l" lean-tissue figure comes from the authors' blog summary and should be checked against the paper.
- A 2025-26 paper titled "Greater glycaemic responses to bodyweight change in South Asians than white Europeans at high risk of type 2 diabetes" appeared in search results (PMC13341349) but its numbers could not be retrieved.
- No data in men aged 35-45; both overfeeding trials studied men of about 19-25.

## 5. Resistance training in South Asians: do they respond differently?

### Takeaway
The claim that South Asians "respond worse" to resistance training for muscle is not supported: the only direct 12-week comparison found no ethnic difference in muscle mass gain, lower-body strength gain or insulin-sensitivity improvement, and muscle protein synthesis rates were not significantly different. Signals of smaller fat-loss, VO2max, blood-pressure and upper-body-strength responses (and slower early squat gains in one 6-week study) come from very small, exploratory studies and are unreplicated. Resistance training clearly improves glycaemia and insulin sensitivity in Asian Indians with type 2 diabetes.

### Cited Findings
- Head-to-head hypertrophy/strength comparison (Alkhayl et al., Sci Rep 2022;12:2469; Glasgow): 18 South Asian and 16 white European men; 12 weeks, 2 sessions/week of resistance training. Free-living muscle protein synthesis was not different: 1.48 ± 0.09 %/day (South Asian) vs 1.59 ± 0.15 %/day (white European), p = 0.522. No ethnic differences in the training response of muscle mass, lower-body strength or insulin sensitivity. Responses in body fat, resting carbohydrate and fat metabolism, blood pressure, VO2max and upper-body strength were less favourable in South Asians. Authors call the study exploratory and say larger studies are needed — [PubMed 35169204](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35169204/); [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8847359/)
- Earlier abstract from the same group (17 South Asian, 12 European men): thigh muscle mass increased significantly (P<0.05) with training, with no difference between ethnic groups (P>0.05) — [Physiological Society abstract](https://www.physoc.org/?p=65701)
- Six-week study in novices (Knox et al., Res Sports Med 2017): 15 Caucasian and 13 South Asian young men, training 3x/week. Both groups improved on all lifts; 3RM squat gains diverged from about session 7 (slower in South Asians), upper-body gains were comparable, and with squat excluded there was no between-group interaction. Authors call the data "novel but provisional" — [PubMed 28412865](https://www.pubmed.gov/28412865/)
- Same cohort, cardiometabolic markers (Knox, Sculthorpe & Grace, F1000Research 2018): no change in insulin, HOMA-IR, triglycerides, ADMA or L-arginine in either group after 6 weeks; authors conclude the cardiometabolic response to resistance exercise is comparable, though the inflammatory response may be prolonged in South Asians — [F1000Research](https://f1000research.com/articles/7-1334/v1)
- Type 2 diabetes, India (Misra et al., Diabetes Care 2008;31:1282-7): 30 Asian Indian patients with type 2 diabetes; 12 weeks of progressive resistance training of six muscle groups, two sets of 10 repetitions. Insulin sensitivity (KITT) rose from 1.22 to 2.13 (P<0.0001); HbA1c fell by 0.54% (P<0.001); fasting glucose fell by 2.7 mmol/L; lipids and truncal and peripheral subcutaneous fat also improved — [PubMed 18316394](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18316394/)
- A second Indian trial (48 untrained patients with type 2 diabetes, 8 weeks, with a control group; N Am J Med Sci 2012) reported an HbA1c change of 0.6 ± 0.5 in the progressive resistance training group vs no change in controls — [PubMed 22912941](https://www.pubmed.gov/22912941/)
- Fitness matters at least as much as fatness: in Ghouri et al. 2013 (100 South Asian, 100 European men aged 40-70), lower VO2max, lower physical activity and greater adiposity individually explained 68%, 29% and 52% of the ethnic difference in insulin resistance, and 83% together; South Asians had lower fitness than Europeans at every level of physical activity — [PubMed 23811809](https://www.pubmed.gov/23811809/); [Glasgow release](https://www.gla.ac.uk/news/archiveofnews/2013/june/headline_281942_en.html)
- Acute exercise in people with non-diabetic hyperglycaemia: reductions in insulin and insulin resistance after low-volume HIIT and continuous moderate exercise were greater in South Asians than white Europeans (crossover trial; aerobic, not resistance) — [Diabetes Care article PDF](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article-pdf/532241/dc201393.pdf)

### Inferences
- UNSUPPORTED: "South Asians are hard-gainers / need a different hypertrophy programme / build less muscle from the same training." The best available direct evidence shows similar muscle and leg-strength gains. Standard evidence-based programming (progressive overload, adequate volume, protein) should be the default.
- WEAK/EXPLORATORY: smaller improvements in body fat, VO2max and blood pressure from resistance training alone in South Asian men (Alkhayl, n=34) and slower early squat progress (Knox, n=28). These would need replication before being used to change a programme.
- PLAUSIBLE PRACTICAL ADJUSTMENT: because cardiorespiratory fitness explained the largest share of the ethnic gap in insulin resistance (Ghouri) and resistance training alone gave smaller VO2max and fat responses, keeping some aerobic conditioning alongside lifting is a sensible default for a South Asian user during a muscle-gain phase. This is an inference from cross-sectional and small-trial data.
- Resistance training has a metabolic rationale in this group beyond appearance (Misra 2008; Lear 2009 fat-to-lean ratio), which an app can use as motivation without overstating it: the Misra trial was in people with diabetes, not healthy lean men.

### Gaps
- Alkhayl 2022 details not retrieved: participant ages and BMI, exact exercise prescription (loads, sets, proximity to failure), protein intake, and the numeric changes per group. Whether the groups were matched for baseline training status is unverified.
- Whether Misra 2008 had a control group was not confirmed (unverified recall: it was a single-arm before-after study). Author names for the 2012 controlled trial were not confirmed.
- No trial compares hypertrophy response by ethnicity beyond 12 weeks, in trained individuals, in men aged 35-45, or with different training doses. No dose-response study of resistance training in South Asians was found.
- No study of protein dose or anabolic response to protein feeding by ethnicity was found.
- Sri Lankan (e.g. SL-DART) and other South Asian resistance-training trials were not searched.

## 6. Physical activity guidance specific to South Asians

### Takeaway
An Indian consensus group recommends 60 minutes of activity every day for healthy Asian Indians (including 15 minutes of muscle strengthening), and two Glasgow cross-sectional studies estimate South Asians need roughly 230-270 minutes/week of moderate activity to match the cardiometabolic risk profile of white Europeans doing 150. These are expert consensus and cross-sectional modelling respectively, not trial-proven targets; major international guidelines do not set ethnicity-specific volumes.

### Cited Findings
- Indian consensus (Misra et al., Diabetes Technol Ther 2012;14:83-98): "A total of 60 min of physical activity is recommended every day for healthy Asian Indians in view of the high predisposition to develop T2DM and CHD", comprising at least 30 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, 15 min of work-related activity and 15 min of muscle-strengthening exercise. Rationale includes data that Asian Indians are more sedentary than white Caucasians — [Misra et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1089/dia.2011.0111)
- Celis-Morales et al., PLoS One 2013: 75 South Asian and 83 European men aged 40-70, accelerometer-measured activity; the moderate-activity level at which South Asian men matched the overall cardiometabolic risk-factor score of European men doing 150 min/week was 266 min/week (95% CI 185-347). Cross-sectional; authors call for prospective replication — [PLoS One 2013 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3859604); [Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/90348)
- Iliodromiti et al. 2016: 148 South Asians and 163 white Europeans aged 18-70 (men and women): South Asians needed 232 min/week (95% CI 200-268) of moderate-equivalent activity for the same risk-factor score as white Europeans doing 150 min/week, i.e. about 10-15 extra minutes per day — [PubMed 27529339](https://www.pubmed.gov/27529339/)
- The AHA 2018 statement stresses culturally tailored lifestyle advice for South Asians (diet and activity) rather than a different activity volume, per its summaries — [AHA release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/685174)

### Inferences
- A lifting programme of 3-5 sessions a week plus daily walking would comfortably meet the Indian consensus 60 min/day and the ~230-270 min/week equivalence figure; the app can present "aim above the generic 150 minutes" as a reasonable South Asian-specific nudge, labelled as consensus/cross-sectional evidence.
- The equivalence studies count aerobic moderate-intensity minutes and risk-factor scores, not disease outcomes, and say nothing about how resistance-training minutes should be counted.

### Gaps
- No prospective or randomised evidence that the higher volume reduces events in South Asians was found.
- UK Chief Medical Officers', WHO 2020 and South Asian Health Foundation positions on ethnicity-specific activity volumes were not retrieved.

## 7. Micronutrients and protein: vitamin D, vitamin B12, protein intake

### Takeaway
Vitamin D deficiency is extremely common in UK South Asians (92% below 50 nmol/L and 55% below 25 nmol/L in UK Biobank), and low B12 is common in Indian adults, particularly vegetarians, though estimates vary widely with the cut-off used. Evidence that correcting vitamin D improves strength is modest and mainly in older, deficient people; there is no evidence it increases muscle mass. Indian dietary guidance sets protein at 0.83 g/kg/day (1 g/kg/day on cereal-based diets), well below typical muscle-gain targets, so a vegetarian South Asian lifter may need to plan protein deliberately.

### Cited Findings
- Vitamin D, UK South Asians: among 6,433 South Asian UK Biobank participants (ages 40-69) with a measurement, 55% (n=3,538) had 25(OH)D <25 nmol/L, 92% (n=5,918) had <50 nmol/L and 20% (n=1,287) had <15 nmol/L; including 824 with undetectable levels, 60% were <25 and 93% <50 nmol/L. Men had lower levels than women (median 21.7 vs 24.3 nmol/L; 58.4% vs 50.4% <25 nmol/L in the conference abstract); Pakistani participants had the lowest medians (19 nmol/L vs 24 Indian and 26 Bangladeshi). Authors caution UK Biobank is not population-representative (Darling et al., Br J Nutr 2020/21) — [Aberdeen research record](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/very-high-prevalence-of-25-hydroxyvitamin-d-deficiency-in-6433-uk/); [Surrey release](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/severe-vitamin-d-deficiencies-uk-south-asian-population-puts-their-health-risk)
- Vitamin D and muscle: meta-analysis of 30 RCTs (5,615 people, mean age 61.1 y) found a small positive effect of vitamin D supplementation on muscle strength, larger in people with baseline 25(OH)D <30 nmol/L and in those aged ≥65, and no significant effect on muscle mass or power (Beaudart et al., J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2014) — [PubMed 25033068](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25033068/). A 2024 review states current evidence does not support beneficial effects of vitamin D supplementation on muscle health overall — [Frontiers in Nutrition 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1450265/pdf)
- Vitamin B12, India: a review of 14 studies in healthy Indian adults and elderly reported deficiency prevalence of 78.5% (adults) and 61.7% (elderly) "based on varying cut offs", higher in vegetarians, and cautions that heterogeneous methods and cut-offs prevent firm conclusions — [Indian J Nutr Diet review](https://informaticsjournals.com/index.php/ijnd/article/view/23083). Other secondary reports cite pooled prevalences of about 47-51% for India; I could not verify the primary sources — [Telangana Today report](https://telanganatoday.com/widespread-vitamin-b12-deficiency-in-india-linked-to-vegetarian-and-vegan-diets)
- Protein requirement, India: ICMR-NIN 2020 sets the protein EAR at 0.66 g/kg/day and RDA at 0.83 g/kg/day, with a note that people on cereal-based diets with low-quality protein should aim for 1 g/kg/day; the recommended cereal:legume:milk ratio moved to 3:1:2.5 (from 11:1:3 in 2010) to improve protein quality — [ICMR-NIN brief note on RDA 2020](https://www.nin.res.in/rdabook/brief_note.pdf)
- Protein intake, India: a secondary summary of ICMR-NIN "What India Eats" (2020) reports urban average protein intake of 55.4 g/day (11.4% of energy), with only 5-18% of urban Indians getting most protein from high-quality sources. Weak source (retailer blog); check the original report — [secondary summary](https://prorganiq.co.in/blogs/new/nutrition-surveys-highlight-low-protein-intake-among-indians)
- Diet pattern: the AHA statement notes many South Asians, including vegetarians, eat diets high in refined carbohydrate and saturated fat (tropical oils) — [AHA release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/685174)

### Inferences
- ESTABLISHED: high prevalence of low vitamin D (UK South Asians) and low B12 (Indian adults, vegetarians). UNCERTAIN: exact B12 prevalence, and how far either applies to a given diaspora individual with a mixed diet.
- For a coach app: suggesting that South Asian users, especially vegetarians and those in high latitudes, ask for 25(OH)D and B12 to be checked alongside the metabolic panel is defensible. Claiming vitamin D "builds muscle" is not; the strength benefit is small and concentrated in deficient, older people, and no effect on muscle mass was found.
- At 55 g/day, a 70 kg man would be at roughly 0.8 g/kg/day (my arithmetic from the cited figure), about the Indian RDA but far below common hypertrophy targets covered by other research notes. Low habitual protein and cereal-dominant protein quality are a more likely practical limiter for a vegetarian South Asian lifter than any ethnic difference in training response. This is an inference; no trial tests it.
- Do not assume diet from ethnicity: the app should ask about dietary pattern (vegetarian, lacto-vegetarian, eggs, meat) rather than infer it.

### Gaps
- Primary nationally representative data on protein intake in g/kg/day for Indian men, and diaspora-specific intake data, were not retrieved (search not executed).
- Vitamin D prevalence within South Asia and in US South Asians (e.g. MASALA) not retrieved.
- B12 prevalence in diaspora South Asian vegetarians, and any evidence linking B12 status to strength or hypertrophy outcomes, not found.
- Bislev et al. 2021 meta-analysis of vitamin D and muscle (reported elsewhere as showing no benefit or small harm) could not be confirmed.
- Creatine response in vegetarians, iron status and other nutrients were out of scope and not searched.

## 8. Frame size and skeletal-muscle norms: do "skinny" South Asian men start from lower baseline muscle?

### Takeaway
On average, yes: Indian reference data put the low-muscle cut-off for men at an appendicular lean mass index of about 6.0-6.1 kg/m², well below the 7.0 kg/m² used by Asian and European sarcopenia definitions, and grip strength in population studies is lowest in South Asia. This describes a lower population starting point (reflecting smaller frames, lifetime nutrition and activity as well as ancestry), not a lower ceiling or a slower response to training.

### Cited Findings
- Sarco-CUBES (Chandigarh, healthy Indian adults; Pal et al., Eur Geriatr Med 2020;11:725-36): cut-offs defined as 2 SD below the mean of a young reference group (20-39 y): DXA appendicular skeletal muscle index <6.11 kg/m² in men and <4.61 kg/m² in women. Prevalence of sarcopenia was higher when European cut-offs were applied — [Sarco-CUBES paper](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s41999-020-00332-z.pdf)
- Pooled Indian analysis (Zengin et al., Calcif Tissue Int 2021): Indian-specific cut-points for men: grip strength 22.93 kg, appendicular lean mass 15.41 kg, ALM index 6.03 kg/m² (women: 10.76 kg, 9.95 kg, 4.64 kg/m²), "lower than existing definitions" — [Zengin et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00223-021-00860-1)
- Comparator: AWGS 2019 defines low muscle mass in men as ALM/height² <7.0 kg/m² (women <5.4) and low grip as <28 kg (women <18) (as tabulated in the Indian literature above) — [Zengin et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00223-021-00860-1)
- Indian DXA dataset: 2,220 healthy Indian adults aged 18-79 (36% women; BMI 14-44) were used to build anthropometric prediction equations for lean body mass and appendicular lean soft tissue; simple equations with age, height and weight explained >90% of variance (Kulkarni et al., J Appl Physiol 2013) — [PubMed 23950165](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23950165/)
- Grip strength: in PURE (125,462 healthy adults aged 35-70, 21 countries), grip strength was lowest in South Asia, South East Asia and Africa; the highest median was 50 kg in men under 40 from Europe/North America (Leong et al., J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle 2016;7:535-46) — [Leong et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcsm.12112)
- Ethnic comparison at matched fat mass: South Asian men had 3.57 kg less lean mass than European men (Lear 2009; section 1) — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/725895)
- Fitness: South Asian men had lower VO2max than European men at all levels of physical activity (Ghouri 2013) — [PubMed 23811809](https://www.pubmed.gov/23811809/)

### Inferences
- A 2-SD cut-off of ~6.1 kg/m² implies the young Indian male reference mean is correspondingly lower than in the reference populations behind the 7.0 kg/m² cut-off; so a lean South Asian man who "looks skinny" is often near the middle of his own reference distribution, not abnormal.
- Lower average baseline does not imply lower trainability: the only direct training comparison found similar muscle gains (section 5). For a beginner, a lower starting point may mean more visible relative progress.
- Western strength standards and "normal" lean-mass charts built on European/North American samples may make a South Asian beginner look further behind than he is; progress should be tracked against his own baseline.
- Reference differences mix genetics with early-life nutrition, protein intake, activity and frame size; they are not evidence of a fixed biological limit. Diaspora men raised with different nutrition may differ from India-based reference samples.

### Gaps
- Mean (not cut-off) ALM index or total lean mass for Indian men aged 30-45 was not retrieved; I found no age-banded adult male reference table (Marwaha's DXA lean-mass charts cover children/adolescents and sportswomen).
- The South Asian male median grip strength in PURE and UK Biobank grip-strength comparisons (e.g. Ntuk 2017) were not retrieved.
- No reference data for Pakistani, Bangladeshi, Sri Lankan or Nepali men, or for diaspora South Asian men by generation, were found.
- No data on frame size (wrist/elbow breadth, bone mass) in relation to muscle-gain potential in South Asian men were found.
