## 1. Weekly volume: how many hard sets per muscle group per week?

### Takeaway
Volume has the clearest dose-response of any variable: more weekly sets produce more hypertrophy with diminishing returns, and position stands converge on roughly 10+ sets per muscle per week as the productive floor for people who want to maximise growth, with 12-20 as a commonly cited range for trained lifters. Strength plateaus at far lower volumes than hypertrophy; the exact "ceiling" numbers from the newest meta-regression (Pelland et al.) are reported inconsistently by secondary sources and need full-text verification.

### Cited Findings
- **Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson, Zourdos — "The Resistance Training Dose Response: Meta-Regressions Exploring the Effects of Weekly Volume and Frequency on Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength Gains", Sports Medicine 56(2):481-505 (2026; online 2025; SportRxiv preprint 2024). (MA)** Multi-level meta-regressions across 67 studies; the posterior probability that the volume slope exceeds zero is 100% for both hypertrophy and strength, with diminishing returns "considerably more pronounced" for strength **[abstract, preprint]** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)
- Pelland et al. counted sets three ways (total, fractional, direct); the "fractional" method (a direct set = 1, an indirect set for a synergist = 0.5) gave the best-fitting models for both outcomes **[abstract + secondary]** — [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [ResearchGate record of published version](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398333408_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_Response_Meta-Regressions_Exploring_the_Effects_of_Weekly_Volume_and_Frequency_on_Muscle_Hypertrophy_and_Strength_Gains_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_ResponseJ_C_Pelland_et)
- Pelland et al. summary table lists a **minimum effective dose of ~4 sets/week** as sufficient for detectable hypertrophy; one weekly set produced a detectable strength gain **[secondary — search snippet of the paper's table and blog summaries]** — [ResearchGate full-text record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/384628335_The_Resistance_Training_Dose-Response_Meta-Regressions_Exploring_the_Effects_of_Weekly_Volume_and_Frequency_on_Muscle_Hypertrophy_and_Strength_Gain); [FitChef summary](https://fitchef.com/studies/training-volume-sets-per-week-study/)
- **[conflict]** Where Pelland's returns flatten is reported differently by different summaries: (a) the same lab's follow-up says "no detectable superiority beyond ~31 fractional sets per week for hypertrophy and ~3 fractional sets per week for strength"; (b) one blog gives a 5-10 set "sweet spot" with accelerating diminishing returns above ~18 sets and a strength plateau at ~4 fractional sets; (c) a chart site reads the curve as ~4% growth at 10 weekly sets, ~8% at 30, still rising at 45. A per-set slope of 0.24% muscle size per added weekly set (95% CrI 0.15-0.33) around a mean of 12.25 fractional sets is cited by one blog; the paper's text also mentions 0.38% per set from an earlier linear model. None of these was verified against the full text — [Remmert et al. per-session preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537); [FitChef](https://fitchef.com/studies/training-volume-sets-per-week-study/); [VBT Coach chart](https://vbtcoach.com/charts/volume-dose-response-hypertrophy); [Anvil Journal](https://anvil.nikam.dev/blog/en/training-frequency-hypertrophy.html)
- **Remmert et al. (2025 preprint, same lab), "Is There Too Much of a Good Thing? Meta-Regressions of the Effect of Per-Session Volume on Hypertrophy and Strength". (MA, preprint)** Positive dose-response between per-session volume and hypertrophy "with diminishing returns – particularly over ~11 fractional sets" per muscle per session; ~2 direct sets per session for strength **[abstract]** — [SportRxiv](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390450237_Is_There_Too_Much_of_a_Good_Thing_Meta-Regressions_of_the_Effect_of_Per-Session_Volume_on_Hypertrophy_and_Strength)
- **Schoenfeld, Ogborn, Krieger (2017), J Sports Sci 35:1073-82. (MA; older than 2018, flagged)** 15 studies / 34 treatment groups; "graded dose-response relationship whereby increases in RT volume produce greater gains in muscle hypertrophy"; sets were binned <5, 5-9, 10+ per muscle per week, and the authors stated 10+ sets produced almost twice the gains of <5; the categorical test was only a trend (P = 0.074), and the data could only inform up to ~10 weekly sets **[abstract + critical commentary]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197); [PainScience critique](https://www.painscience.com/biblio/statistically-insignificant-evidence-that-more-is-better-at-the-gym.html)
- **Baz-Valle et al. (2022), J Hum Kinet 81:199-210. (SR + small MA)** Trained young men, 7 studies (6 in the quantitative analysis); categories low <12, moderate 12-20, high >20 weekly sets; no significant moderate-vs-high difference for quadriceps (p = 0.19) or biceps (p = 0.59), high volume better for triceps (p = 0.01); conclusion: "12-20 weekly sets per muscle group may be an optimum standard recommendation" for young trained men **[abstract]** — [PMC8884877](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877)
- **ACSM 2026 Position Stand (Currier et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 58(4):851-872) — umbrella review of 137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants. (Consensus built on SRs)** "Muscle hypertrophy was enhanced by higher volumes (≥10 sets/wk) and eccentric overload"; strength was enhanced by "2-3 sets" per exercise **[abstract]** — [Newswise/ACSM release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years); [Ovid/MSSE](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/acsm-msse/fulltext/10.1249/mss.0000000000003897~american-college-of-sports-medicine-position-stand); [ACSM Science Spotlight](https://acsm.org/science-spotlight-acsm-releases-new-position-stand-on-resistance-training/)
- **IUSCA Position Stand (Schoenfeld, Fisher, Grgic, Haun, Helms, Phillips, Steele, Vigotsky 2021, Int J Strength Cond 1(1)). (Consensus)** "A dose of approximately 10 sets per muscle per week" as a working dose; cap per-session volume near ~10 sets and, where applicable, increase weekly frequency to distribute additional volume **[search snippets of the stand]** — [IUSCA journal](https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/view/81)
- **Ralston et al. (2017), Sports Medicine (strength). (MA; older, flagged)** 9 studies / 61 treatment groups comparing low, medium and high weekly sets per exercise; "graded dose-response relationship between weekly sets and strength gain" **[abstract]** — [UWS record](https://research-portal.uws.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effect-of-weekly-set-volume-on-strength-gain-a-meta-analysis/)
- **Currier et al. (2023), Br J Sports Med 57(18):1211-1220 — Bayesian network MA, 178 strength studies (n=5097) and 119 hypertrophy studies (n=3364). (MA)** All prescriptions beat no exercise; "the highest-ranked prescriptions for hypertrophy included multiple sets"; all prescriptions "comparably promoted muscle hypertrophy" **[abstract]** — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/18/1211.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- App rule candidates (derived, not directly stated by any one source): minimum effective ≈ 4 fractional sets/muscle/week (Pelland); default productive target ≈ 10 sets/muscle/week (ACSM 2026, IUSCA); intermediate range 12-20 (Baz-Valle); above ~20 only as an optional, monitored specialisation for one or two muscles, because the incremental gain per set shrinks and the evidence above 20 is thin and inconsistent.
- Count sets fractionally (direct = 1, indirect = 0.5) when tallying per-muscle volume — this is the best-supported counting method and prevents over-prescribing arm/shoulder work in programmes heavy on presses and rows.
- A per-session cap of about 10-11 fractional sets per muscle (IUSCA; Remmert) gives a mechanical rule for when to split volume across more days.
- "Hard set" should mean a set taken to roughly 0-3 reps in reserve (see section 4); most volume studies used sets at or near failure, so warm-up sets must not count.

### Gaps
- Pelland et al.'s exact published thresholds (minimum effective dose, the 18-set vs 31-set flattening point, per-set percentage gains) could not be read from the full text; secondary sources conflict. Verify against [the paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w) before hard-coding numbers.
- The often-quoted Schoenfeld 2017 figures (0.37% extra growth per additional weekly set; 5.4% / 6.6% / 9.8% gains for <5 / 5-9 / 10+ sets) were not confirmed by any source reached in this session.
- A 2026 non-peer-reviewed re-analysis says Pelland's hypertrophy subset is 220 effects from 35 studies, so the hypertrophy evidence is smaller than the "67 studies" headline — [preprints.org](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202610.1022). Not verified against the paper.
- Whether novices need less volume than intermediates is not cleanly quantified in the sources reached; Baz-Valle covers trained men only.

## 2. Frequency: does 2x/week beat 1x when volume is equated? Split implications

### Takeaway
For hypertrophy, frequency matters little once weekly volume is equated; its main value is as a way to distribute volume and keep per-session sets productive. For strength, higher frequency shows a more consistent (but still volume-linked) benefit. Split choice is therefore a scheduling decision, not a growth lever.

### Cited Findings
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic, Krieger (2019), J Sports Sci — "How many times per week should a muscle be trained to maximize muscle hypertrophy?" (MA)** 25 studies; no significant difference between higher and lower frequency on a volume-equated basis; pooled effect of 13 volume-equated trials cited as 0.07 (95% CI −0.02 to 0.17); in non-volume-equated studies, higher frequency was favoured with a modest difference between 1 and 3+ days/week **[secondary; effect size from a non-peer-reviewed preprint citing the paper]** — [Henselmans review of the paper](https://mennohenselmans.com/training-frequency-2018-meta-analysis-review/); [preprints.org](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202610.1022); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906)
- **Pelland et al. (2026) (MA)**: for hypertrophy, the posterior probability for a frequency effect is below 100%, "indicating compatibility with negligible effects"; for strength it is 100%, with diminishing returns. "Only strength demonstrates consistently identifiable effects with frequency" **[abstract, preprint]** — [SportRxiv](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [sponet record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)
- **Grgic et al. (2018), Sports Medicine 48(5):1207-20 (strength). (MA)** Effect sizes rose 0.74, 0.82, 0.93, 1.08 for 1, 2, 3, 4+ sessions/week, but in the volume-equated subgroup the frequency effect was not significant (p = 0.421) **[abstract, via secondary]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x); [Henselmans](https://mennohenselmans.com/training-frequency-2018-meta-analysis-review/)
- **Ralston et al. (2018), Sports Medicine - Open (strength). (MA)** With volume equated, low (1 day/week) and high (≥3 days/week) frequency produced similar strength gains; high frequency is a practical way to raise weekly volume **[abstract]** — [PMC6081873](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6081873)
- **Ramos-Campo et al. (2024), J Strength Cond Res — split vs full-body routines. (MA)** 14 studies, 392 subjects, 4-12 week programmes: "the use of Sp or FB routines within a resistance training program does not significantly impact either strength gains or muscle hypertrophy when volume is equated" **[abstract]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38595233/); [Biolayne summary](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-25/full-body-vs-split-routine-wars/)
- **IUSCA Position Stand (2021) (Consensus)**: the retrieved snippet reads "...benefit to greater weekly per-muscle training frequencies provided set volume is equated" with the start of the sentence cut off, so whether the stand affirms or denies a benefit could not be confirmed (the 2019 meta-analysis by the same lead author found none); the stand does say frequency should be used to distribute volume when per-session sets exceed ~10 **[truncated search snippet — verify]** — [IUSCA journal](https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/view/81)
- **ACSM 2026 (Consensus)**: strength was enhanced by "≥2 sessions/wk"; the headline public message is to train all major muscle groups at least twice a week **[abstract + press]** — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years); [McMaster News](https://news.mcmaster.ca/consistency-over-perfection-new-resistance-training-guidelines-say/)
- **Currier et al. (2023) network MA**: top-ranked strength prescription was higher-load, multiset, thrice-weekly (SMD 1.60, 95% CrI 1.38-1.82 vs control); top-ranked hypertrophy prescription was higher-load, multiset, twice-weekly (0.66, 0.47-0.85), though all prescriptions were comparable for hypertrophy **[abstract]** — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/18/1211.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- Default rule: hit each muscle ≥2x/week (ACSM 2026) unless the user's weekly volume is low enough to fit in one session (≤ ~10 sets per muscle).
- Split mapping (inference from the volume-distribution logic, not a tested comparison): **3 days/week → full body x3** (each muscle 3x; ~3-6 sets per muscle per session reaches 10-18/week); **4 days/week → upper/lower x2** (each muscle 2x; ~5-10 sets per muscle per session); **5 days/week → upper/lower + push/pull/legs hybrid, or PPL plus two full/upper-lower days**, so every muscle still gets ~2 exposures. A strict once-through PPL on 3 days gives 1x frequency and forces all weekly volume for a muscle into one session, which collides with the ~10-11 sets/session cap at higher volumes.
- For a strength goal, practise the target lifts 2-3x/week (Pelland frequency signal; Currier thrice-weekly ranking).
- Because full-body and split routines are equivalent when volume is matched, the app can let schedule, session length and preference choose the split without a performance penalty.

### Gaps
- Could not read the Schoenfeld 2019 abstract directly; its numbers come via secondary and preprint citations.
- Henselmans argues some "volume-equated" studies were set-equated but not repetition- or effort-equated, so the "frequency doesn't matter" conclusion is contested at the margins — [Henselmans](https://mennohenselmans.com/training-frequency-2018-meta-analysis-review/).
- No direct RCT comparison of specific named splits (full body vs U/L vs PPL) at 3/4/5 days was found beyond the split-vs-full-body meta-analysis.

## 3. Rep ranges and load

### Takeaway
Hypertrophy is essentially load-independent when sets are taken to or near failure; maximal (1RM) strength is load-dependent and favours heavy loading (roughly ≥80% 1RM). The classic "hypertrophy zone" of 8-12 reps is a convenient middle, not a physiological requirement.

### Cited Findings
- **Lopez et al. (2021), Med Sci Sports Exerc 53(6):1206-1216 — network MA, 28 studies, 747 healthy adults, all sets to volitional failure. (MA)** Low load (>15RM), moderate (9-15RM) and high (≤8RM) produced no meaningful hypertrophy difference; strength was superior with high and moderate vs low load; conclusion: hypertrophy "appears load independent" **[abstract]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/)
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn, Krieger (2017), J Strength Cond Res 31(12):3508-3523 — 21 studies, low (≤60% 1RM) vs high (>60% 1RM), sets to momentary failure. (MA; 2017, flagged)** 1RM gains significantly greater with high load; no difference in isometric strength; hypertrophy similar between conditions **[abstract]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/)
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic, Van Every, Plotkin (2021), Sports 9(2):32 — "Loading Recommendations... A Re-Examination of the Repetition Continuum". (Narrative review)** Muscular adaptations "can be obtained, and in some cases optimized, across a wide spectrum of loading zones"; the literature does support a "strength zone" for 1RM; the heavy-load advantage "dissipates when testing is carried out on a modality different than that used in the study training program" **[abstract]** — [PMC7927075](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7927075/)
- **ACSM 2026 (Consensus)**: "Voluntary strength was enhanced by lifting heavier loads (≥80% one-repetition maximum), through a complete range of motion, for 2-3 sets, at the beginning of training sessions, and ≥2 sessions/wk" **[abstract]** — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)
- ACSM 2026, per secondary summaries only: acceptable hypertrophy load range spans 30-100% 1RM; power is trained at 30-70% 1RM with fast concentrics **[secondary — unverified against the paper]** — [BodyMotionLab summary](https://bodymotionlab.com/blog/acsm-resistance-training-guidelines-2026); [getfitcraft summary](https://getfitcraft.com/science/acsm-2026-resistance-training-guidelines)
- **Currier et al. (2023) network MA**: higher-load (>80% 1RM) prescriptions ranked highest for strength; "the highest-ranked prescriptions for strength involved higher loads" **[abstract]** — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/18/1211.full.pdf)
- **Grgic (2020) MA on fibre types**: no significant difference between low- and high-load training for type I or type II fibre hypertrophy, with very wide confidence intervals **[abstract via search]** — [J Hum Kinet](https://johk.pl/?p=5649)
- **ACSM 2009 Position Stand (Med Sci Sports Exerc 41(3):687-708) (Consensus; superseded, flagged)**: untrained lifters train major muscles with total-body workouts 2-3 days/week; novices gain similarly from single- and multiple-set programmes **[secondary summary]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/); [IDEA summary](https://www.ideafit.com/progression-models-in-resistance-training-for-healthy-adults)

### Inferences
- Hypertrophy programming can use any rep target from roughly 5 to 30 provided sets end near failure; the lower bound for load in the cited meta-analyses is "low load" defined as >15RM or ≤60% 1RM, and secondary summaries of ACSM 2026 put the floor at ~30% 1RM.
- Practical rep ranges by exercise type are **expert convention, not meta-analytic findings**: e.g. heavier ranges (about 5-10) for multi-joint barbell lifts where technique and systemic fatigue limit very high reps, moderate (about 8-15) for machines/dumbbell compounds, and higher (about 10-20+) for single-joint isolation work where joint stress under heavy load is the limiter. Treat these as defaults a user can override.
- Strength goal: main lifts mostly at ≥80% 1RM (about 1-6 reps), tested on the lift you train (specificity), 2-3 sets per exercise as the evidence-based floor, placed first in the session (ACSM 2026).
- Low-load work is a legitimate substitution for home/limited-equipment users or sore joints, at the cost of more discomfort per set and weaker 1RM transfer.

### Gaps
- The IUSCA stand's explicit rep-range recommendation was not retrieved.
- No meta-analysis reached specifies rep ranges by exercise type; this remains expert practice.
- The widely repeated "~30% 1RM minimum load" threshold was only found in secondary summaries of ACSM 2026, not in a primary abstract.
- How much heavy (≥80%) work is needed per week to maximise 1RM is explicitly called an open question by Schoenfeld et al. 2021 — [PMC7927075](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7927075/).

## 4. Proximity to failure, reps in reserve (RIR) and velocity loss

### Takeaway
Training closer to failure gives slightly more hypertrophy per set, but going all the way to momentary failure is not required and costs more fatigue; strength gains are essentially flat across a wide RIR range. Expert disagreement is about how much the last 1-3 reps matter, with the 2026 ACSM stand saying failure "did not consistently impact" outcomes while the Robinson meta-regression finds a real dose-response for size. Velocity loss tracks proximity to failure well on average but poorly for individuals.

### Cited Findings
- **Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, Jukic, Steele, Zourdos (2024), Sports Medicine 54(9) — series of exploratory multilevel meta-regressions. (MA)** Strength: confidence intervals for the estimated-RIR marginal slope contained zero in all best-fit models — "strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR". Hypertrophy: negative marginal slope with CIs excluding zero — "muscle hypertrophy improves as sets are terminated closer to failure". Models adjusted for load, volume-equating method, intervention duration and training status. Authors stress the analysis is exploratory because RIR was *estimated* from study descriptions **[abstract]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2); [Semantic Scholar abstract](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Exploring-the-Dose%E2%80%93Response-Relationship-Between-to-Robinson-Pelland/21e9295d1d3c1f07fe73d9d9796410ecae4a508f); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/295)
- University press release for Robinson et al.: for strength, stop "about 3-5 reps short of failure"; for size, work closer to failure **[secondary — press-release wording, not an abstract finding]** — [FAU news](https://www.fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/muscle-growth-strength-study)
- **Refalo et al. (2023), Sports Medicine 53(3):649-665 — 15 studies. (SR + MA)** Any-definition "set failure" vs non-failure: trivial advantage for failure, ES 0.19 (95% CI 0.00-0.37, p = 0.045); sub-analysis of *momentary muscular failure* vs non-failure showed no advantage; authors suggest a possible non-linear relationship between proximity to failure and hypertrophy **[abstract]** — [Deakin repository](https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Influence_of_Resistance_Training_Proximity-to-Failure_on_Skeletal_Muscle_Hypertrophy_A_Systematic_Review_with_Meta-analysis/22030796); [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/78679612-6ff4-4d00-95d1-7920d072a760/full)
- **Refalo et al. (2024), J Sports Sci 42(1):85-101 — RCT (within-subject), 18 resistance-trained adults, 8 weeks, 2 sessions/week. (RCT)** Quadriceps thickness increase was 0.181 cm (HDI 0.119-0.243) training to momentary failure vs 0.182 cm (HDI 0.115-0.247) at 1-2 RIR; failure "consistently induces higher levels of acute neuromuscular fatigue" **[abstract]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/)
- **Grgic, Schoenfeld, Orazem, Sabol (2022), J Sport Health Sci 11(2):202-211 — 15 studies. (MA)** Failure vs non-failure: strength ES −0.09 (95% CI −0.22 to 0.05), hypertrophy ES 0.22 (−0.11 to 0.55), neither significant; when volume was not equated, non-failure was better for strength (ES −0.32; −0.57 to −0.07); in resistance-trained subjects failure slightly favoured hypertrophy (ES 0.15; 0.03-0.26) **[abstract]** — [VU repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/42420/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/dc36ba7ccd994798964f66b93bd8f662)
- **ACSM 2026 (Consensus)**: "Training to momentary muscle fatigue... did not consistently impact training outcomes"; secondary summaries say adequate effort can be reached about 2-3 reps short of failure **[abstract + secondary]** — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years); [getfitcraft](https://getfitcraft.com/science/acsm-2026-resistance-training-guidelines)
- **Jukic et al. (2023), Sports Medicine 53(1):177-214 — 18 acute + 19 longitudinal studies on velocity-loss (VL) thresholds. (SR + MA)** VL did not influence strength or muscular-endurance gains; higher VL was associated with more hypertrophy (b = 0.006; 95% CI 0.001-0.012) and worse jump, sprint and submaximal-velocity outcomes (CMJ b = −0.040; 95% CI −0.079 to −0.001); graded relationship between VL in a set and acute volume, neuromuscular, metabolic and perceptual responses **[abstract]** — [PMC9807551](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9807551); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40279-022-01754-4)
- **Pareja-Blanco et al. (2017), Scand J Med Sci Sports — squat, 20% vs 40% velocity loss, 8 weeks. (RCT)** VL20 produced similar squat strength gains to VL40 and greater CMJ improvement (9.5% vs 3.5%) while performing ~40% fewer repetitions; VL40 gave greater vastus lateralis/intermedius hypertrophy **[abstract]** — [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/sms.12678); [Loyola record](https://www.uloyola.es/en/research-transfer/publications/effects-of-velocity-loss-during-resistance-training-on-athletic-performance-strength-gains-and-muscle-adaptations-article)
- Follow-up squat work with 0/10/20/40% thresholds: VL20 and VL40 both increased muscle size but the group×time interaction was not significant (P = 0.06); the 20% group showed the largest strength gains and the 40% group the largest (non-significantly) CSA gain. Bench press study with 0/15/25/50% thresholds: greater VL gave more hypertrophy, moderate VL was more favourable for strength **[search-level abstract summaries; individual records not opened]** — [Biolayne summary](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-50/velocity-loss-in-the-squat-more-isnt-always-better/); [bench press paper PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Effects%20of%20velocity%20loss%20in%20the%20bench%20press%20exercise%20on%20strength%20gains%2C%20neuromuscular%20adaptations%2C%20and%20muscle%20hypertrophy.pdf)
- Later VBT literature summarises thresholds as: low VL (<25%) preferred for power and maximal strength, higher VL (>25%, close to failure) for hypertrophy; Weakley's 2020 squat study compared 10%, 20% and 30% thresholds **[secondary citation inside a 2025 PeerJ paper]** — [PeerJ 2025](https://peerj.com/articles/19797); [Weakley 2020 manuscript](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/download/1d63960ee760a11862500b396fd4c7673346354ab7e6205fa0398a223efd2c91/243348/AM_Weakley_2020_The_effects_of_10_20_and.pdf)
- **Rodríguez-Rosell et al. (2020), J Strength Cond Res 34(9):2537-2547. (Observational)** Bench press and full squat to failure at 50-80% 1RM: very strong relationship between relative velocity loss and the percentage of possible repetitions completed (R² ≈ 0.97 bench, 0.93 squat), with prediction equations for RIR; reps-to-failure varied widely between people (CV ~15-22% bench, 26-34% squat); VL at a given relative load was larger for bench than squat **[abstract]** — [JSCR](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2020/09000/relationship_between_velocity_loss_and_repetitions.18.aspx); [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/med/31045753)
- **Counter-evidence on velocity as an RIR gauge**: a 2023 Eur J Sport Sci study found bar velocity at 3-0 RIR was unreliable for bench press (ICC 0.40) and concluded velocity "does not accurately assess repetitions-in-reserve"; a 2025 PeerJ study found only a moderate link between mean velocity and perceived RIR (average r² ≈ 0.3), modified by exercise, load, VL threshold and set number **[abstract]** — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17461391.2023.2235314); [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/19797)
- **Jukic et al. (2023), Sports Medicine - Open 9:80 — 46 trained people. (Primary study)** The same VL threshold produced variable acute responses across individuals; VL thresholds "cannot be used to reliably control the amount of work completed"; sex, training status and personality traits affected results **[abstract]** — [Abertay record](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/one-velocity-loss-threshold-does-not-fit-all-consideration-of-sex/)

### Inferences
- Default effort rule for hypertrophy: most sets at about 1-3 RIR; allow 0 RIR mainly on the last set of low-risk isolation/machine exercises. This captures most of Robinson's proximity benefit while avoiding the extra fatigue Refalo measured at failure.
- Strength rule: heavy sets can stop further from failure (roughly 2-5 RIR) with no measurable cost to 1RM gains (Robinson; Jukic's VL result), preserving bar speed and technique.
- For a camera/sensor-based coach: average relationship between velocity loss and reps left is strong, so a set-termination trigger is defensible (about 10-20% VL for strength/power, ~25-40% for hypertrophy), but individual and exercise variation is large — calibrate per user and per lift rather than hard-coding one threshold, and present the estimate as approximate.
- Novices' RIR estimates are likely less accurate, so the app should bias beginners toward occasional "test to technical failure" calibration sets on safe exercises (inference; no accuracy data retrieved in this session).

### Gaps
- Robinson et al.'s numeric slope (extra growth per rep closer to failure) was not retrieved.
- RIR self-rating accuracy literature (e.g. by training status or rep range) was not searched.
- The mapping "X% velocity loss ≈ Y RIR" per exercise from Rodríguez-Rosell's equations was not retrieved in numeric form.
- One outlier secondary site claims ACSM 2026 "explicitly requires" proximity to failure, contradicting the abstract; treat as an error — [search summary of secondary sites](https://bodymotionlab.com/blog/acsm-resistance-training-guidelines-2026).

## 5. Rest between sets

### Takeaway
Rest long enough to keep set quality: more than ~60 seconds gives a small hypertrophy advantage, with no clear extra benefit beyond ~90 seconds in the pooled data, while maximal strength work is conventionally rested longer (about 2-5 minutes). The evidence base is small and effects are small.

### Cited Findings
- **Singer et al. (2024; Schoenfeld co-author), "Give it a rest", Front Sports Act Living 6:1429789 — Bayesian MA, 9 studies / 19 measurements. (SR + MA)** "A small hypertrophic benefit to employing inter-set rest interval durations >60 s, perhaps mediated by reductions in volume load"; no appreciable hypertrophy differences detected when resting >90 s; short-vs-long central estimates: arm 0.13 (95% CrI −0.27 to 0.51), thigh 0.17 (−0.13 to 0.43), whole body −0.08 (−0.45 to 0.29); training to failure or not did not meaningfully change the interaction **[abstract]** — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/18363f4031e0487793db2084bee23f38)
- **Schoenfeld et al. (2016), J Strength Cond Res 30(7):1805-1812 — 21 resistance-trained men, 8 weeks, 1-min vs 3-min rest. (RCT; pre-2018, flagged)** "Longer rest periods promote greater increases in muscle strength and hypertrophy in young resistance-trained men" **[abstract]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/)
- **Grgic et al. (2017), Eur J Sport Sci — SR on rest and hypertrophy (flagged, pre-2018)**: longer rests are not detrimental to growth and may be advantageous, particularly in trained individuals **[secondary]** — [ResearchGate](https://researchgate.net/profile/Brad_Schoenfeld/publication/317832672_The_effects_of_short_versus_long_inter-set_rest_intervals_in_resistance_training_on_measures_of_muscle_hypertrophy_A_systematic_review/links/59a010ad0f7e9b18359db18f/The-effects-of-short-versus-long-inter-set-rest-intervals-in-resistance-training-on-measures-of-muscle-hypertrophy-A-systematic-review.pdf)
- **de Salles et al. (2009), Sports Medicine — review (old, flagged)**: resting 3-5 minutes between sets produced greater increases in absolute strength **[abstract]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19691365/)
- **ACSM 2009 (superseded)**: 2-3 minutes' rest between multiple-set bouts **[secondary]** — [IDEA summary](https://www.ideafit.com/progression-models-in-resistance-training-for-healthy-adults)
- **IUSCA 2021 (Consensus)**: notes the traditional 30-90 s hypertrophy guidance and states longer rest intervals are likely needed when sets are taken to muscular failure **[search snippets; exact recommendation wording not confirmed]** — [IUSCA journal](https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/view/81)
- **Iversen et al. (2021), Sports Medicine 51(10):2079-2095 — "No Time to Lift?" (Narrative review)**: supersets, drop sets and rest-pause roughly halve training time versus traditional training while maintaining volume **[abstract]** — [PMC8449772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772)

### Inferences
- App defaults (derived): isolation/single-joint and machine accessories ~60-120 s; multi-joint hypertrophy sets ~2-3 min; heavy strength sets (≥80% 1RM) ~3-5 min. The compound-vs-isolation distinction is practice-based: the Singer meta-analysis did not report a compound/isolation split in the material retrieved.
- A minimum rest floor (~60 s) is better supported than a precise optimum; let users rest longer if reps drop sharply set to set.
- For time-poor users, pair antagonist exercises (supersets) rather than cutting rest below ~60 s.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis reached quantifies rest by exercise type (compound vs isolation).
- The Grgic et al. 2018 strength-rest review's "2-5 min" range could not be confirmed from a primary source.
- ACSM 2026's conclusion on rest intervals was not retrieved (the review covered rest, but no summary gave the finding).

## 6. Range of motion, lengthened partials, stretch-mediated hypertrophy

### Takeaway
Full ROM is a safe default with trivial-to-small advantages over generic partials; what seems to matter is loading the muscle at long lengths. Early studies (mostly untrained) favoured lengthened partials, but 2025 trials in trained lifters found them equivalent to full ROM, not superior. Static stretching alone produces only small growth.

### Cited Findings
- **Wolf, Androulakis-Korakakis, Fisher, Schoenfeld, Steele (2023), Int J Strength Cond 3(1) — Bayesian multilevel MA. (MA)** Full vs partial ROM overall SMD 0.12 (95% CI −0.02 to 0.26) favouring full ROM (trivial); all outcome-specific SMDs between 0.05 and 0.2; sub-group analysis suggested a possible hypertrophy benefit for partials performed at long muscle lengths vs full ROM (−0.28; 95% CI −0.81 to 0.16 — interval crosses zero); ROM specificity for strength; conclusion: "a full or long ROM" may enhance most outcomes **[abstract]** — [IUSCA journal](https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/view/182)
- **Wolf et al. (2025), PeerJ 13:e18904 — within-participant RCT, 30 resistance-trained people, upper limbs. (RCT)** "Lengthened partial repetitions elicit similar muscular adaptations as full range of motion repetitions during resistance training in trained individuals" **[abstract]** — [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/18904)
- **Multi-site cluster-randomised trial (2025)** across 15 training sites, full ROM (n = 134) vs lengthened partials (n = 163), low-volume twice-weekly programme, 12 weeks: similar changes in muscle size and estimated strength **[secondary; author attribution not verified]** — [Biolayne summary](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-34/again-lengthened-partials-appear-comparable-to-full-rom-training/); [Planfit blog](https://planfit.ai/en/blog/lengthened-partials)
- **Pedrosa et al. (2022), Eur J Sport Sci 22(8):1250-1260 — leg extension, 12 weeks. (RCT)** Four conditions (full ROM; initial/lengthened partial; final/shortened partial; varied); lengthened partials produced equal or greater quadriceps hypertrophy than full ROM, shortened partials lagged **[secondary]** — [getfitcraft summary](https://getfitcraft.com/science/lengthened-partials-research); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/351538891_Partial_range_of_motion_training_elicits_favorable_improvements_in_muscular_adaptations_when_carried_out_at_long_muscle_lengths)
- **Kassiano et al. (2023), J Strength Cond Res — calf raise. (RCT)** Initial-range (lengthened) partials gave greater medial gastrocnemius hypertrophy than full ROM, with no between-group difference for the lateral gastrocnemius **[abstract/review]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/365127382_Greater_gastrocnemius_muscle_hypertrophy_after_partial_range_of_motion_training_performed_at_long_muscle_lengths); [2025/26 review, PMC12951042](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12951042/)
- **Maeo and colleagues**: lengthened-position triceps cable-extension training produced greater long-head triceps hypertrophy than full ROM; initial-ROM hip extension produced greater gluteus maximus growth than full ROM **[secondary via review]** — [PMC12951042](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12951042/)
- **Static stretching meta-analysis (2024), Sports Medicine - Open (Warneke group, attribution per secondary source). (MA)** Chronic static stretching ≥2 weeks: small hypertrophy effect d = 0.20; long-duration subgroup (≥15 min) d = 0.28; described as "of minor effectiveness"; no significant moderator in meta-regression **[abstract]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38637473/); [PMC11026323](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11026323/)
- A second 2024 multilevel meta-analysis found longer stretch durations and greater exercise variety associated with more hypertrophy; an earlier review concluded passive low-intensity stretching most likely does not promote hypertrophy **[abstract + secondary; conflicting]** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-024-00772-y); [Biolayne](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-30/static-stretching-for-growth/)
- **ACSM 2026**: strength is enhanced by training "through a complete range of motion" **[abstract]** — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)

### Inferences
- Form-coaching rule: cue a full, controlled ROM with emphasis on reaching the stretched position; do not penalise users for cutting the shortened (lockout) end slightly, but flag reps that cut the lengthened end.
- Exercise-selection rule: prefer variants that load the muscle at long length where equipment allows (e.g. overhead triceps work, seated-style hamstring curls, deep calf raises) — supported by individual RCTs, not yet by meta-analysis.
- Lengthened partials are an optional intensifier (e.g. after reaching full-ROM failure) rather than a replacement; in trained lifters they match but do not beat full ROM.
- Stretching between sets or as a standalone growth method is too weak (d ≈ 0.2 with long durations) to prioritise in a time-limited app programme.

### Gaps
- Specific percentages often quoted (e.g. Kassiano's 15.2% vs 6.7% gastrocnemius growth; Maeo's "~1.5-fold" overhead-extension advantage) were not confirmed in this session.
- The sign convention of Wolf 2023's −0.28 subgroup effect is ambiguous in the abstract.
- The 2025 PeerJ trial's lengthened-partial arm reportedly lifted higher volume load, a possible confound — [search summary of PeerJ 18904](https://peerj.com/articles/18904).
- Most lengthened-partial evidence covers single-joint exercises and few muscles; generalisation to all compound lifts is untested.

## 7. Tempo

### Takeaway
Rep tempo has little effect on hypertrophy across a broad range; avoid extremely slow reps, control the lowering phase, and lift with intent. No optimal eccentric duration has been established.

### Cited Findings
- **Schoenfeld, Ogborn, Krieger (2015), Sports Medicine — 8 studies. (MA; 2015, flagged)** "Hypertrophic outcomes are similar when training with repetition durations ranging from 0.5 to 8 s"; very slow training (>10 s per repetition) appears inferior for hypertrophy, with limited controlled evidence **[abstract]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25601394/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0304-0)
- **Enes et al. (2025), J Strength Cond Res 39(12):1331-1339 — 14 studies with other variables controlled. (MA)** Repetition tempo has little overall effect on hypertrophy; the concentric phase showed slightly better results with a faster tempo; eccentric tempo differences were essentially negligible; authors suggest prioritising volume and proximity to failure **[secondary — not confirmed against the paper]** — [SET FOR SET summary](https://www.setforset.com/blogs/news/new-research-on-tempo-training-and-rep-speed); [BoxRox summary](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=253503)
- **Amdi & King (2025), J Sports Sci (SportRxiv 2024) — eccentric phase duration specifically** — exists; conclusions not retrieved **[existence only]** — [SportRxiv PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/439/925/868)
- **ACSM 2026**: "time under tension... did not consistently impact training outcomes"; "eccentric overload" (heavier eccentric loading, a different thing from slow eccentrics) enhanced hypertrophy **[abstract]** — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)

### Inferences
- App rule: accept any rep lasting roughly 2-8 s total; coach a controlled eccentric (about 1-3 s, mainly for technique, safety and consistent ROM tracking) and a deliberate, reasonably fast concentric. Do not count time under tension as a progression variable.
- For strength and power goals, cue maximal intended concentric speed (consistent with the velocity-loss literature in section 4).
- Flag reps slower than ~10 s as counter-productive unless the user chose a tempo variation deliberately.

### Gaps
- The 2025 meta-analysis details (effect sizes, whether Bayesian) rest on blog summaries only.
- No source reached gives a validated "optimal" eccentric duration; Amdi & King's findings need retrieval.

## 8. Progression and deloads

### Takeaway
Adding reps and adding load are both effective ways to progress, which supports double progression as a default. Planned deloads are a coaching convention with consensus support but almost no experimental support: the one RCT found a mid-programme week off did not help hypertrophy and slightly hurt strength.

### Cited Findings
- **Plotkin et al. (2022), PeerJ 10:e14142 — RCT, 43 trained adults, 8 weeks, lower body 2x/week, load progression (reps fixed) vs repetition progression (load fixed). (RCT)** "Both progressions of repetitions and load appear to be viable strategies"; 1RM squat slightly favoured load progression (~2.0 kg, of questionable practical significance); rectus femoris growth modestly favoured the reps group; other differences very small. Caveats: starting range was 8-12 reps and sets were taken to failure **[abstract + commentary]** — [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/14142); [Stanford Lifestyle Medicine commentary](https://lifestylemedicine.stanford.edu/increasing-weight-or-increasing-reps-can-both-make-you-stronger/)
- **ACSM 2026 (Consensus)**: periodization and set structure "did not consistently impact training outcomes" for the average healthy adult; complex periodization is not required **[abstract]** — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years); [ACSM slide deck](https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Pronouncement-ppt-deck_resistance-training-ps.pdf)
- **Coleman et al. (2024), PeerJ 12:e16777 — RCT, 39 trained adults (29 men, 10 women), 9-week programme, one group took a full week off at the midpoint. (RCT)** No appreciable differences in lower-body muscle size, local endurance or power; continuous training produced greater isometric and dynamic lower-body strength and slightly better readiness-to-train scores; authors: a one-week deload "appears to negatively influence measures of lower body muscle strength but has no effect on lower body hypertrophy, power or local muscular endurance" **[abstract]** — [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/16777); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)
- **Bell et al. (2023), Sports Medicine - Open 9:87 — international Delphi, 34 → 21 expert coaches over 3 rounds. (Consensus)** Deloading defined as "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training"; the panel agreed deloads can be pre-planned, autoregulated, or both, without ranking them **[abstract + secondary]** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0)
- **Rogerson et al. (2024), Sports Medicine - Open — survey of 246 competitive strength/physique athletes. (Cross-sectional survey)** Typical deload lasted 6.4 ± 1.7 days, every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks; volume fell (fewer reps per set and fewer sets per week), frequency unchanged, load and effort reduced (more RIR), exercise selection maintained **[abstract]** — [SHU repository](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/33446)
- **Bell, Darragh, Kyle, Rogerson, Nolan (2025) — practical recommendations (Narrative/consensus)**: deloads are typically implemented by reducing effort, volume, duration or frequency, commonly pre-planned every 4-8 weeks, typically 5-7 days long **[abstract/secondary]** — [SHU repository](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/35313/)

### Inferences
- **Double progression rule (derived from Plotkin's equivalence, not directly tested as a named model)**: prescribe a rep range (e.g. 8-12); add reps session to session at the same load until the top of the range is reached on all sets at the target RIR, then increase load by the smallest available increment and return to the bottom of the range. For strength lifts, prioritise load progression within a narrow low-rep range, since load progression slightly favoured 1RM.
- Novices can progress load most sessions; intermediates will mostly progress reps first. (Expert convention; no quantified source retrieved.)
- **Deload rule**: do not force a full week off. Default to *reactive* deloads triggered by stalled or regressing performance across ~2 sessions, persistent soreness/joint pain, or low readiness; offer an optional *planned* lighter week every 4-8 weeks (cut sets by roughly a third to a half and add 2+ RIR while keeping exercises and frequency), reflecting practice in Rogerson/Bell. Present this as convention, not proven benefit. The size of the volume cut is an inference — the surveyed athletes reported reducing volume but the percentage was not retrieved.
- Progressive overload should be framed as an outcome of adaptation (reps/load rise at the same RIR), not a schedule to force by sacrificing form or RIR targets.

### Gaps
- No RCT tests "double progression" against other named progression models; no source reached gives ACSM 2009's "2-10% load increase" rule (could not be confirmed).
- Only one deload RCT exists and it tested complete cessation for a week, not a reduced-volume deload; long-term (>9 weeks) effects on fatigue, injury or adherence are unstudied.
- Bell et al. 2023's specific consensus statements on deload frequency and volume reduction were not retrieved.
- Volume-progression studies (e.g. progressively adding weekly sets) were not searched.

## 9. How programming changes by goal

### Takeaway
The same building blocks apply to every goal; what changes is which variable is prioritised: volume and proximity to failure for size, load and specificity for strength, maintained training stimulus plus a moderate deficit and high protein for fat loss, and simple twice-weekly whole-body consistency for general fitness.

### Cited Findings

**Build muscle (hypertrophy)**
- ≥10 sets/muscle/week enhances hypertrophy (ACSM 2026); 12-20 sets suggested for trained young men (Baz-Valle 2022); load is flexible when sets are near failure (Lopez 2021); growth improves as sets end closer to failure (Robinson 2024) — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years); [PMC8884877](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877); [PubMed 33433148](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)

**Maximal strength**
- ACSM 2026: ≥80% 1RM, full ROM, 2-3 sets, strength exercises at the beginning of the session, ≥2 sessions/week **[abstract]** — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)
- Currier 2023 network MA: top strength prescription was higher-load (>80% 1RM), multiset, thrice-weekly (SMD 1.60 vs control) **[abstract]** — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/18/1211.full.pdf)
- Strength gains plateau at low volume (about 3-4 fractional sets/week per the same lab's summaries; ~2 direct sets per session) and are insensitive to proximity to failure **[secondary + abstract]** — [Remmert preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537); [Robinson DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)
- Specificity: heavy-load advantage for strength disappears when tested on a different modality than trained (Schoenfeld 2021); ROM-specific strength gains (Wolf 2023) **[abstract]** — [PMC7927075](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7927075/); [IUSCA journal](https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/view/182)
- **Androulakis-Korakakis et al. (2021), Front Sports Act Living — powerlifters (n = 25 intervention, 57 survey). (Mixed methods)** Suggested effective low-dose structure: 3-6 sets per lift per week, 1-5 reps, >80% 1RM, RPE 7.5-9.5, in 6-12 week phases; single heavy reps at RPE 9-9.5 as the bare minimum, with less reliable gains **[secondary summary]** — [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/pdf); [MTI summary](https://mtntactical.com/knowledge/research-review-minimum-training-dose-for-1rm-strength-improvement-in-trained-lifters/)

**Fat loss while preserving muscle**
- **Murphy & Koehler (2022), Scand J Med Sci Sports — MA + meta-regression. (MA)** Lean-mass gains from resistance training were impaired in an energy deficit vs energy balance (ES −0.57, p = 0.02); strength gains were not significantly affected (ES −0.31, p = 0.28); a deficit of ~500 kcal/day was estimated to abolish lean-mass gains; authors advise avoiding deficits >500 kcal/day when preserving lean mass. Mostly untrained participants; trials 3-28 weeks **[abstract]** — [TUM record](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/node?id=1632530); [authors' summary](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/can-i-gain-muscle-and-lose-weight-at-the-same-time)
- **Roth, Schoenfeld, Behringer (2022), Eur J Appl Physiol — SR, 15 studies in resistance-trained athletes under caloric restriction. (SR)** Studies using ≥10 weekly sets per muscle group showed little to no lean-mass loss (mostly women); studies that increased volume during the diet tended to lose little or no lean mass versus those that reduced it; evidence judged insufficient to say higher volume is clearly better **[abstract]** — [PMC9012799](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9012799/); a secondary summary reads it more strongly as "volume does not influence lean mass retention in trained males" — [Biolayne](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-6/save-your-muscle-while-dieting/)
- **Helms, Aragon, Fitschen (2014), J Int Soc Sports Nutr — narrative review for natural bodybuilders (2014, flagged; lean, advanced population). (Consensus/narrative)** Protein 2.3-3.1 g/kg lean body mass/day, fat 15-30% of calories; in athletes, weekly loss of 0.7% vs 1.4% of bodyweight over 4-11 weeks produced fat-mass reductions of 31% vs 21% and lean mass +2.1% vs unchanged — slower loss is better for lean-mass retention **[abstract/full text via search]** — [PMC4033492](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492)
- **Bickel et al. (2011), Med Sci Sports Exerc — 70 adults, 16 weeks of 3 days/week training then 32 weeks at reduced dose. (RCT; 2011, flagged)** One-third and one-ninth of the original dose both preserved muscle size in young adults (one-third produced further myofibre hypertrophy) but not in older adults **[abstract]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/)
- **Spiering et al. (2021), J Strength Cond Res — brief review. (Narrative)** "Exercise intensity seems to be the key variable for maintaining physical performance over time, despite relatively large reductions in exercise frequency and volume" **[abstract]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972)

**General fitness / health minimums**
- **WHO 2020 Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour. (Guideline)** Adults should do muscle-strengthening activities at moderate or greater intensity involving all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week (strong recommendation, moderate-certainty evidence), plus 150-300 min moderate or 75-150 min vigorous aerobic activity weekly; "doing some physical activity is better than doing none" **[guideline text]** — [WHO via NCBI Bookshelf](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK566048/)
- **ACSM 2026 (Consensus)**: any amount of resistance training improves strength, size, power and function versus none; the primary goal for most adults is regular participation, training all major muscle groups at least twice weekly; equipment type, exercise complexity, set structure, time under tension, blood-flow restriction and periodization did not consistently change outcomes **[abstract + press]** — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years); [McMaster News](https://news.mcmaster.ca/consistency-over-perfection-new-resistance-training-guidelines-say/); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1119943)
- **Behm, Granacher, Warneke, Aragão-Santos, Da Silva-Grigoletto, Konrad (2024), Sports Medicine 54(2):289-302 — "Minimalist Training". (Narrative review)** One weekly session with fewer than 3 sets and loads below 50% 1RM may be enough for strength gains in beginners; heavier loads tend to be superior; multi-joint exercises give similar or larger effects than single-joint **[abstract]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37924459/)
- **Iversen et al. (2021), Sports Medicine — "No Time to Lift?" (Narrative review)**: for time-limited trainees, weekly volume matters more than frequency; supersets/drop sets/rest-pause roughly halve session time at maintained volume **[abstract]** — [PMC8449772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772)
- **ACSM 2009 (superseded)**: untrained lifters, total-body sessions 2-3 days/week; single- and multiple-set programmes give similar early gains **[secondary]** — [IDEA summary](https://www.ideafit.com/progression-models-in-resistance-training-for-healthy-adults)

### Inferences
Goal templates for the app (derived by combining the findings above; each number traces to the cited source in brackets):

| Variable | Build muscle | Maximal strength | Fat loss, keep muscle | General fitness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly sets per muscle | ~10-20 fractional sets; start ~10, add when recovering [ACSM 2026; Baz-Valle] | Lower: a few hard sets per main lift per session, 2-3 sets per exercise [ACSM 2026; Remmert] plus accessory hypertrophy work | Keep pre-diet volume if recoverable, aim ≥10 sets [Roth]; never below roughly one-third of prior volume [Bickel] | ≥4 sets as a floor [Pelland, secondary]; all major muscle groups [WHO] |
| Frequency | Each muscle ~2x/week [ACSM 2026] | Each main lift 2-3x/week [Currier; Pelland] | Same as build | ≥2 days/week [WHO; ACSM 2026] |
| Load / reps | Any, ~5-30 reps near failure [Lopez] | ≥80% 1RM, low reps, lifts first in session [ACSM 2026] | Keep loads heavy — intensity maintains adaptations [Spiering] | Moderate loads; multi-joint exercises [Behm] |
| Effort | ~0-3 RIR [Robinson; Refalo] | Further from failure acceptable [Robinson] | Same RIR targets; expect rep drops | 2-3 RIR is sufficient [ACSM 2026, secondary] |
| Rest | >60 s, ~1.5-3 min [Singer] | ~3-5 min [de Salles; Schoenfeld 2016] | Same as build | 1-2 min or supersets [Iversen] |
| Nutrition context | Not in scope here | Not in scope here | Deficit ≤ ~500 kcal/day [Murphy & Koehler]; slower loss (~0.7%/week) spares lean mass [Helms]; high protein [Helms] | Not in scope here |

- In a deficit the training goal shifts from "progress" to "hold performance": the success metric is maintaining load × reps at the same RIR, and deload/volume-reduction triggers should be more sensitive.
- For general fitness, adherence is the main lever; a 2-day full-body plan of roughly 6-8 multi-joint exercises for 2-3 sets meets WHO and ACSM minimums and exceeds the minimum effective dose for growth.
- Strength users still benefit from hypertrophy-style accessory volume, because size contributes to long-term strength; this is expert reasoning, not a finding retrieved here.

### Gaps
- ACSM 2026's full recommendation tables (rest, load range for hypertrophy, power prescription, any "minimum dose" statement) were not readable; several details rest on low-quality secondary summaries that disagree with each other.
- The 2020/2021 Sports Medicine meta-analysis on minimum effective dose for 1RM in trained men (often summarised as a single set of 6-12 reps at ~70-85% 1RM, 2-3x/week) could not be confirmed in this session.
- Fat-loss evidence is mostly from untrained people (Murphy & Koehler) or lean physique athletes (Helms, Roth); direct evidence for overweight novice lifters' ideal training volume in a deficit was not found.
- "0.5-1% bodyweight loss per week" is a common recommendation but was not found stated in the sources reached; only the 0.7% vs 1.4% comparison was confirmed.
- Protein figures for non-lean recreational lifters, cardio/concurrent-training interference, and older-adult adjustments were outside this search.
