## 1. Does the hypertrophy response decline by the late 30s/40s? Is the difference meaningful before 60?

### Takeaway
For a man of 38 the direct evidence says no meaningful penalty: the one trial that isolates 35-50-year-old men found the same 8-week lean-mass gain as 18-22-year-olds, and a 111-study meta-regression in men found age did not explain differences in muscle gain. A blunted response does appear in some data from the mid-40s onward (45-55 group in one 20-week trial) and is clearer after 60, so "no difference at 38, possibly emerging in the late 40s, real by the 60s" is the honest summary.

### Cited Findings
- `[TRIAL]` Kerksick et al. 2009 (J Strength Cond Res): 8-week, 4-day/week upper/lower split, college-aged men (18-22 y, n = 24) vs middle-aged men (35-50 y, n = 25). DXA lean mass rose in both: college-aged +0.9 ± 2.4 kg, middle-aged +1.1 ± 1.9 kg (time effect p < 0.001, no age difference). Fat mass diverged: college-aged +0.5 ± 1.3 kg, middle-aged -0.5 ± 1.1 kg (group x time p = 0.01). Both groups increased bench-press and leg-press 1RM. Caveat from the abstract: some values are mean ± SEM, so the ± figures may not be SDs. — [Kerksick et al. 2009, PubMed 19387379](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19387379/)
- `[META]` Benito et al. 2020 (Int J Environ Res Public Health 17(4):1285), healthy adult **males only**: 111 studies, 158 groups, 1,927 participants. Pooled whole-body muscle-mass gain from resistance training +1.53 kg (95% CI 1.30-1.76). In meta-regression, age, body weight, height and programme duration did **not** explain variation in muscle gain; the only significant covariate was sets per workout, with a negative interaction (more sets per session associated with less gain). — [Benito et al. 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/4/1285/xml)
- `[TRIAL]` Lowndes et al. 2009 (J Strength Cond Res), 826 men and women aged 18-39 (mean 24.34 ± 5.69 y), 12 weeks of supervised unilateral arm training: age was **not** associated with the training-induced change in muscle size or isometric strength; age was negatively associated with change in 1RM (r = -0.217, p < 0.001), which the authors called small relative to the training effect. Before training, age correlated positively with cross-sectional area (r = 0.191). — [Lowndes et al. 2009, PMC4103410](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4103410)
- `[TRIAL]` Ahtiainen et al. 2016 (AGE/GeroScience), men and women of different ages: muscle size changed +4.8 ± 6.1% (range -11 to +30%) and strength +21.1 ± 11.5% (range -8 to +60%); "age and sex did not affect the RT responses". — [Ahtiainen et al. 2016, PubMed 26767377](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26767377/)
- `[TRIAL]` Phillips et al. 2017 (JCI Insight), 20 weeks of supervised whole-body training in young (18-28 y, n = 11), middle-aged (45-55 y, n = 20) and older (65-75 y, n = 17) adults: only the young group significantly gained muscle, with a negative correlation between age and muscle growth; acute muscle-protein-synthesis increases were enhanced only in the young; young and middle-aged (not older) reduced adiposity. The authors state that some negative characteristics of ageing are "apparent in midlife". **This conflicts in direction with Kerksick 2009**, but its middle-aged band starts at 45, not 35. — [Phillips et al. 2017, JCI Insight](https://insight.jci.org/articles/view/95581)
- `[TRIAL]` Mayhew et al. 2009 (J Appl Physiol): 21 young and 15 older adults, 16 weeks; gains in muscle mass, type II fibre size and strength were similar in young and old. — [Mayhew et al. 2009, PMC2777794](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2777794)
- `[TRIAL]` Kosek et al. 2006: 3 days/week training gave more robust myofibre hypertrophy in young than in older adults, particularly among men. — [Kosek et al. 2006, PubMed 16614355](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16614355/)
- `[TRIAL]` Ivey et al. 2000: no significant difference in muscle-volume response to strength training between young and older men and women. — [Ivey et al. 2000, PubMed 11078093](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11078093/)
- `[TRIAL]` Bickel et al. 2011: older adults needed a higher weekly maintenance dose than young adults to retain the hypertrophy gained in training. — [Bickel et al. 2011, PubMed 21131862](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/)
- `[META]` Straight et al. 2020 (J Appl Physiol), 35 studies, **participants aged 59.0-88.5 y only**: resistance training increased MHC I fibre size (ES +0.51) and MHC II fibre size (ES +0.81); within this older range, age was negatively associated with fibre-size change. Does not transfer directly to a 38-year-old. — [Straight et al. 2020, PMC7473942](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7473942)
- `[TRIAL]` Lixandrão et al. 2016 (J Strength Cond Res): **older** adults (training n = 6, control n = 8), 10 weeks of leg press; vastus lateralis CSA was significantly increased only after 18 sessions / 9 weeks (+7.1%). Very small study in older adults; the "slower than young" comparison is against other studies, not a concurrent young group. — [Lixandrão et al. 2016, PubMed 26110345](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26110345/)

### Inferences
- A 38-year-old sits inside the Kerksick middle-aged band (35-50) and the Lowndes sample (18-39) and below the Phillips middle-aged band (45-55). The app can honestly say: "At 38 your muscles respond to training about as well as a 20-year-old's in the studies that tested it."
- The conflict between Kerksick (no age effect at 35-50, 8 weeks) and Phillips (no significant gain at 45-55, 20 weeks, n = 20) is unresolved; both are small. A reasonable reading is that any decline begins somewhere in the mid-to-late 40s and is small relative to between-person variability (see section 6).
- Kerksick's middle-aged men lost fat while gaining lean mass, whereas the college-aged men gained fat. For a lean ("skinny") 38-year-old this recomposition effect is less relevant than for a typical middle-aged trainee carrying more fat.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis specifically comparing hypertrophy in 35-45-year-olds against under-30s was found. The claim rests on one 8-week trial (Kerksick), one arm-only cohort that stops at 39 (Lowndes), and meta-regression (Benito).
- Reviews on masters athletes were not retrieved (search budget exhausted).
- The age at which population muscle mass starts to decline (commonly attributed to Janssen et al. 2000, J Appl Physiol) could not be verified in this session, so no figure is given.
- Ages of the "older" group in Mayhew 2009 and Kosek 2006 were not shown in the extracts; both are understood to be 60+ and should be checked.

## 2. Recovery: does it slow in the 35-45 range, and what programming adjustments are evidence-based?

### Takeaway
The evidence is mixed and thin. One small trial found trained men of about 40 lost more force and recovered more slowly after a hard squat session than trained men of about 22, while a 2025 meta-analysis of 36 studies found no greater muscle-damage symptoms with age. Being trained mattered at least as much as age for soreness. There are no trials that test different volumes or frequencies specifically in 35-45-year-olds; adjustments at this age are coaching judgment.

### Cited Findings
- `[TRIAL]` Fernandes, Lamb & Twist 2019 (Sports 7(6):132): 10 x 10 squats at 60% 1RM in trained young (mean 22.3 y), trained middle-aged (39.9 y) and untrained middle-aged (44.4 y) men, n = 9 per group; measures at 24 h and 72 h. Trained middle-aged men showed moderately larger declines in maximal voluntary contraction, resting doublet force and squat peak power than trained young men; differences in soreness, creatine kinase and voluntary activation were "unclear". Untrained middle-aged men had greater soreness and peak-power loss than trained middle-aged men. Authors' conclusion: middle-aged males "experienced greater symptoms of muscle damage and an impaired recovery profile than young resistance trained males". — [Fernandes et al. 2019, PMC6628445](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6628445)
- `[META]` Fernandes et al. 2025 (Journal of Aging and Physical Activity), 36 studies: change in muscle function at 24 h, 72 h and peak did not differ between older and younger adults; soreness was greater in **younger** adults for all comparisons; creatine kinase was greater in younger adults at 24 h and at peak (SMD = -0.32); a sex relationship with peak muscle-function change was evident for males (SMD = -0.45). Conclusion: "Advancing age is not associated with greater symptoms of EIMD." — [Lancaster University repository record](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/227837)
- `[SECONDARY]` The university press release for that meta-analysis describes the older group as aged 35 and over versus 18-25-year-olds. The paper's own age cut-offs were not confirmed. — [Middlesex University press release, April 2025](https://www.mdx.ac.uk/news/2025/4/muscle-damage-study/)
- `[META]` A 2023 systematic scoping review of recovery from resistance exercise in adults aged 65+ found "considerable variability in study protocols and inconsistency in findings"; it does not cover middle age. — [Recovery from Resistance Exercise in Older Adults, PMC10317890](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10317890/)
- `[META]` Benito et al. 2020: more sets per workout was associated with less muscle gain in men (the only significant moderator). — [Benito et al. 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/4/1285/xml)
- `[META]` Iversen et al. 2021 (Sports Medicine 51(10):2079-2095): advises restricting warm-up to exercise-specific warm-up sets and stretching only if flexibility is a goal. Not age-specific. — [Iversen et al. 2021, PubMed 34125411](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/)

### Inferences
- The same research group produced both the 2019 trial (slower recovery at ~40) and the 2025 meta-analysis (no age effect). The trial used an extreme, unaccustomed protocol (100 squats) in 9 men per group; the meta-analysis pools many protocols. For app purposes: do not tell a 38-year-old he "recovers slowly"; do tell him the first weeks will produce soreness because he is untrained, and that this fades with training (untrained middle-aged men were sorer than trained middle-aged men).
- `[COACHING RULE OF THUMB]` Practical age-appropriate defaults that are consistent with, but not proven by, the above: start with moderate per-session volume and add sets only if recovery allows; spread weekly volume over 2-4 sessions rather than one very long session (supported indirectly by Benito's sets-per-workout finding); use ramp-up sets of the working exercise as the warm-up.
- 72 h was not long enough for full force recovery in the trained ~40-year-olds after a very damaging session, so leaving roughly 48-72 h before training the same muscle hard again is a defensible default. This is an inference from one small trial, not a tested prescription.

### Gaps
- No trial was found comparing training frequencies, volumes or exercise selections in 35-45-year-old men.
- No evidence was retrieved on warm-up duration and injury or performance specifically at age ~40.
- Sleep, stress and occupational load as modifiers of recovery in this age band were not researched.
- Exact 72 h values from Fernandes 2019 were not available in the extracts.

## 3. Injury: commonest resistance-training injuries, risk factors, prevention, tendon vs muscle timelines

### Takeaway
Weight training has a low injury rate per hour compared with team sports (bodybuilding-style training about 0.24-1 injury per 1,000 hours); the shoulder, lower back/spine and knee are the usual sites, and most emergency-department cases are dropped weights and sprains/strains with free weights. Tendon stiffness adapts more slowly than muscle strength and needs sustained high loading, which is the physiological argument for conservative load progression in the first months. Evidence on risk factors and prevention is weak and mostly retrospective.

### Cited Findings
- `[META]` Keogh & Winwood 2017 (Sports Medicine 47(3):479-501): bodybuilding had the lowest injury rate, 0.24-1 injury per 1,000 hours (0.12-0.7 injuries per lifter per year); strongman 4.5-6.1 and Highland Games 7.5 per 1,000 hours were highest. Authors conclude weight-training sports have "relatively low rates of injury compared with common team sports". Most included studies were retrospective. — [Keogh & Winwood 2017, Bond University record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-epidemiology-of-injuries-across-the-weight-training-sports/)
- `[META]` Aasa et al. 2017 (Br J Sports Med 51(4):211), 9 studies: injury incidence 2.4-3.3 per 1,000 training hours in weightlifting and 1.0-4.4 per 1,000 hours in powerlifting; only one retrospective study had analysed risk factors. `[SECONDARY]` spine, shoulder and knee were the most common sites. — [Aasa et al. 2017, PubMed 27707741](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27707741/)
- `[META]` 2024 update of that review: weightlifting incidence still 2.4-3.3 per 1,000 hours; commonest sites knee, lower back, shoulder, hands/fingers; in powerlifting incidence was low but prevalence high when injury is defined as pain that impairs training. — [Injuries in weightlifting and powerlifting: an updated systematic review, PubMed 39650568](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39650568/)
- `[TRIAL/EPIDEMIOLOGY]` Kerr, Collins & Comstock 2010 (Am J Sports Med), US emergency departments 1990-2007: 25,335 sampled cases, projecting to about 970,801 nationally. — [Kerr et al. 2010, PubMed 20139328](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20139328/)
- `[SECONDARY]` From the same study: upper trunk 25.3% and lower trunk 19.7% of injuries; sprains/strains 46.1%; free weights involved in 90.4%; dropping a weight on oneself was the top mechanism at 65.5%. — [HealthDay summary of Kerr et al. 2010](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/exercise-and-fitness/epidemiology-of-weight-training-injuries-analyzed-637660.html)
- `[META - narrative]` Kolber et al. 2010 (J Strength Cond Res 24(6):1696-1704): up to 36% of documented resistance-training injuries and disorders occur at the shoulder complex; risk factors grouped as intrinsic (joint and muscle imbalances) and extrinsic (improper attention to exercise technique); most evidence retrospective, with "a paucity of research" on predictive variables. — [Kolber et al. 2010, NSUWorks](https://nsuworks.nova.edu/hpd_pt_facarticles/92)
- `[TRIAL]` Kubo et al. 2010 (J Strength Cond Res), 3 months of isometric knee-extensor training: strength rose 29.6% after 2 months and 40.5% after 3 months; on detraining, muscle CSA fell back to baseline at 1 month and tendon stiffness at 2 months. Authors conclude that "the adaptations of tendon properties and muscle morphology to resistance training are slower than those of muscle function". — [Kubo et al. 2010, PubMed 19996769](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19996769/)
- `[META]` Bohm, Mersmann & Arampatzis 2015 (Sports Medicine - Open), healthy adults aged 18-50, interventions of 8 weeks or longer, 27 studies / 37 interventions / 264 participants: training increased tendon stiffness (SMD 0.70, 95% CI 0.51-0.88); adaptation depended significantly on loading intensity, not on contraction type. — [Bohm et al. 2015, PMC4532714](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4532714/)
- `[SECONDARY]` A newsletter summary of Bohm 2015 states loads above 70% of 1RM gave the best tendon adaptation; not confirmed against the paper. — [SportsMedU summary](https://sportsmedu-newsletter.beehiiv.com/p/glimpse-tendons-adapt-exercise)
- `[REVIEW]` Mersmann, Bohm & Arampatzis 2017 (Frontiers in Physiology): muscle and tendon adaptation "does not necessarily proceed in a uniform manner"; an imbalance (muscle strength outpacing tendon capacity) is proposed as a contributor to tendinopathy. The review concerns **youth athletes**; transfer to a 38-year-old novice is by analogy only. — [Mersmann et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2017.00987/full)

### Inferences
- Illustrative arithmetic (my calculation from Keogh & Winwood's range): at 3-4 hours of training per week (150-200 h/year) and 0.24-1 injuries per 1,000 hours, the expected rate is roughly 0.04-0.2 injuries per year, i.e. about one injury every 5-25 years of training. The app can frame injury as uncommon, not as a reason to avoid lifting.
- Because the largest share of emergency-department injuries came from dropped free weights, basic handling rules (collars, controlled re-racking, safety pins or a spotter for bench press and squat, not loading beyond what can be controlled) address the most frequent acute mechanism.
- `[COACHING RULE OF THUMB]` Since neural strength gains arrive in weeks while tendon properties lag (Kubo) and need months of sustained loading (Bohm's minimum inclusion was 8 weeks), a novice's strength can rise faster than connective tissue tolerance. Small, regular load increases and avoiding large jumps in weekly volume during the first 3 months are prudent. No trial retrieved here tests a specific percentage progression rate against injury outcomes.
- Shoulder is the priority joint for prevention in a programme that emphasises pressing, deltoids and lats (see section 8): balance pressing with pulling volume and coach technique, in line with Kolber's intrinsic/extrinsic risk factors.

### Gaps
- No injury-rate data specific to recreational gym-goers aged 35-45 were found; the per-hour figures are from competitive weight-training sports.
- No age-stratified risk for the 35-45 band was retrieved. (Kerr et al. reported only that adults 55+ were injured more often on machines.)
- Elbow/tendon overuse (tendinopathy) incidence in lifters, and the age profile of distal biceps and pectoralis major tendon ruptures, could not be searched (budget exhausted). These are widely described as middle-age-male injuries but no figure is given here.
- The ACSM 2009 progression position stand (commonly cited for a 2-10% load increase rule) could not be verified in this session.
- No randomised evidence was found that any specific warm-up, technique cue or progression rule reduces resistance-training injuries.

## 4. Hormones: testosterone decline by 38, whether it limits muscle gain, when to see a doctor

### Takeaway
Average testosterone falls slowly with age (roughly 0.8-1.6% per year for total testosterone in one large cohort) and a healthy 38-year-old is normally still well inside the young-adult reference range (264-916 ng/dL). Within the normal range, circulating hormone levels did not predict who gained more muscle in training studies of young men. Testing is warranted only when there are symptoms, using two fasting morning samples.

### Cited Findings
- `[COHORT]` Feldman et al. 2002 (J Clin Endocrinol Metab), Massachusetts Male Aging Study, 1,709 men enrolled 1987-89 with 1,156 followed 7-10 years later (a middle-aged cohort): total testosterone declined cross-sectionally at 0.8% per year of age; free and albumin-bound testosterone at about 2% per year; the within-person longitudinal decline was steeper, 1.6% per year for total and 2-3% per year for bioavailable testosterone. Apparent good health (no chronic illness, prescription medication, obesity or excess drinking) added 10-15% to the level of several androgens. — [Feldman et al. 2002, PubMed 11836290](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11836290/)
- `[COHORT/MODEL]` Kelsey et al. 2014 (PLoS One), 13 studies of healthy men: mean total testosterone peaks at 15.4 nmol/L at about age 19 and falls to 13.0 nmol/L by age 40, with no further fall in the mean thereafter but increasing variance; 1st-99th centile range at age 35 was 4.7-29.2 nmol/L. A correction to the reference table was published (PMC4319902). — [Kelsey et al. 2014, PMC4190174](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4190174/)
- `[COHORT]` Travison et al. 2017 (J Clin Endocrinol Metab), 9,054 men from four cohorts: harmonised reference range for healthy non-obese men aged 19-39 is 264-916 ng/dL (2.5th, 5th, 50th, 95th and 97.5th percentiles: 264, 303, 531, 852, 916 ng/dL). — [Travison et al. 2017, PubMed 28324103](https://www.pubmed.gov/28324103/)
- `[TRIAL]` Morton et al. 2018 (Frontiers in Physiology), 49 resistance-trained young men, 12 weeks: no circulating hormone measured pre- or post-exercise, pre- or post-intervention, was consistently associated with changes in fibre size or lean mass. Intramuscular androgen receptor content was higher in the 10 highest than the 10 lowest responders and related linearly to lean-mass change (P < 0.01). Secondary analysis; association, not causation. — [Morton et al. 2018, PubMed 30356739](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30356739/)
- `[TRIAL]` A 2013 PLoS One study of 23 young men reported that post-exercise rises in circulating hormones were unrelated to hypertrophy, while muscle androgen-receptor protein changes were related. — [PMC3793973](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3793973)
- `[GUIDELINE]` Endocrine Society 2018 (Bhasin et al., J Clin Endocrinol Metab): diagnose hypogonadism only in men with symptoms and signs of testosterone deficiency **and** unequivocally and consistently low serum testosterone; use a fasting early-morning total testosterone as the initial test and repeat it to confirm; add free testosterone when total is borderline or SHBG is altered; measure LH and FSH if confirmed low; the guideline recommends against routine screening of the general male population. — [Endocrine Society guideline page](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/testosterone-therapy)

### Inferences
- Illustrative arithmetic (mine, from Kelsey's means): 15.4 to 13.0 nmol/L between ages 19 and 40 is about a 16% lower mean. Using Travison's 19-39 range, a typical 38-year-old remains far above the 264 ng/dL lower limit.
- Because variation in circulating testosterone within the normal range did not predict hypertrophy in young men, the modest age-related shift by 38 is unlikely to be a meaningful limiter in a healthy man. This is an extrapolation: the hormone-hypertrophy studies cited used young (and in Morton's case already-trained) men, not 38-year-old novices.
- Feldman's finding that good health adds 10-15% suggests lifestyle factors the app can influence (avoiding obesity and excess alcohol) matter about as much as a decade of ageing. That comparison is my inference.
- App wording for "when to see a doctor": persistent symptoms suggestive of testosterone deficiency should prompt a GP visit and two fasting morning blood tests; a single low reading is not a diagnosis; the app should not suggest testosterone therapy.

### Gaps
- The specific symptom list in the Endocrine Society guideline (for example low libido, erectile dysfunction, loss of body hair, low energy) was not captured in the extracts and should be copied from the guideline itself.
- A secondary source states about 30% of men with an initial low result are normal on repeat testing; this was not confirmed in the guideline text.
- The AUA guideline threshold and the European Male Ageing Study rates were not retrieved.
- No study was found that directly relates baseline testosterone within the normal range to hypertrophy in men aged 35-45.

## 5. Realistic rate of natural muscle gain for novices; coaching models; FFMI limits

### Takeaway
Trial data are far more modest than popular coaching models: across 111 studies in men the pooled gain was about 1.5 kg of muscle mass per study, 35-50-year-old novices gained 1.1 kg of lean mass in 8 weeks, and the best group in a 9-month supervised trial gained 3.3 kg. The McDonald and Aragon models (about 9-11 kg in year one) are practitioner estimates of best-case potential, not trial results. An FFMI of about 25 is an observed upper bound in one 1995 sample of non-steroid-users, not a proven biological ceiling.

### Cited Findings
**Trial and meta-analytic data**
- `[META]` Benito et al. 2020, healthy adult men, 111 studies, 1,927 participants: pooled muscle-mass gain +1.53 kg (95% CI 1.30-1.76); programme duration did not explain variance in the meta-regression. — [Benito et al. 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/4/1285/xml)
- `[TRIAL]` Kerksick et al. 2009: 8 weeks, middle-aged men 35-50 y, DXA lean mass +1.1 ± 1.9 kg; college-aged +0.9 ± 2.4 kg. — [Kerksick et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19387379/)
- `[TRIAL]` Volek et al. 2013: non-resistance-trained men and women, 96 supervised workouts over about 9 months; lean body mass gain 3.3 ± 1.5 kg (whey), 2.3 ± 1.7 kg (carbohydrate), 1.8 ± 1.6 kg (soy). Mixed-sex sample, so the male-only figure would be somewhat higher. — [Volek et al. 2013, PubMed 24015719](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24015719/)
- `[TRIAL]` Hartman et al. 2007 (Am J Clin Nutr): 56 healthy young novice men, 5 days/week for 12 weeks; fat- and bone-free mass increased in all groups, more with milk than soy or carbohydrate. The kg values were not available in the extract. — [DairyReporter summary of Hartman et al. 2007](https://www.dairyreporter.com/Article/2007/08/14/milk-helps-weightlifters-increase-lean-body-mass/)
- `[TRIAL]` DeFreitas et al. 2011: 25 sedentary men, 8 weeks of high-intensity training, thigh muscle CSA +13.9 cm2 (+9.60%). — [DeFreitas et al. 2011, Eur J Appl Physiol](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-011-1905-4)
- `[TRIAL]` Ahtiainen et al. 2016: mean muscle-size change +4.8 ± 6.1% (range -11 to +30%). — [Ahtiainen et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26767377/)
- `[META]` Morton et al. 2018 (Br J Sports Med), 49 RCTs, 1,863 participants: protein supplementation added +0.30 kg (95% CI 0.09-0.52) fat-free mass on top of training; no further benefit above about 1.6 g/kg/day; the effect diminished with age (coefficient -0.01 kg, 95% CI -0.02 to -0.00, p = 0.002) and was larger in trained people (+0.75 kg, 0.09-1.40). — [Morton et al. 2018, PubMed 28698222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)
- `[REVIEW]` Slater et al. 2019 (Frontiers in Nutrition): "the specific energy surplus required to facilitate muscle hypertrophy is unknown". — [Slater et al. 2019, PMC6710320](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)

**Coaching models (not trial data)**
- `[COACHING RULE OF THUMB]` Lyle McDonald model, as reported by multiple secondary sources: year 1 of proper training 20-25 lb of muscle (about 2 lb/month), year 2 about 10-12 lb, year 3 about 5-6 lb. — [RNT Fitness summary](https://www.rntfitness.co.uk/realistic-rates-muscle-gain/); [Legion Athletics summary](https://legionathletics.com/newbie-gains)
- `[COACHING RULE OF THUMB]` Alan Aragon model, as reported by secondary sources: beginners gain about 1-1.5% of body weight per month, intermediates 0.5-1%, advanced 0.25-0.5%. Secondary sources are not fully consistent (some quote 1-2 lb/month or 2-4 lb/month for beginners). — [BodySpec summary](https://www.bodyspec.com/blog/post/how_fast_can_you_gain_muscle); [RNT Fitness summary](https://www.rntfitness.co.uk/realistic-rates-muscle-gain/)
- `[COACHING RULE OF THUMB, in a peer-reviewed narrative review]` Iraki, Fitschen, Espinar & Helms 2019 (Sports): recommend a diet about 10-20% above maintenance energy with a target body-weight gain of about 0.25-0.5% of body weight per week for novice/intermediate bodybuilders; note that untrained individuals may gain muscle faster than trained ones. This is a **body-weight** gain target, not a muscle gain rate. — [Iraki et al. 2019, PMC6680710](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710)

**FFMI**
- `[TRIAL - cross-sectional]` Kouri, Pope, Katz & Oliva 1995 (Clin J Sport Med): compared FFMI in users and non-users of anabolic steroids and is the origin of the "about 25" natural upper bound. `[SECONDARY]` The sample is described as 157 athletes, 83 users and 74 non-users (sources disagree on the description), including men up to about 17% body fat; the 25 value was the approximate upper bound observed among non-users, a descriptive cohort boundary, not a diagnostic test. — [Kouri et al. 1995, PubMed 7496846](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7496846/); [MuscleEvo discussion](https://muscleevo.net/?p=15935)
- `[TRIAL - cross-sectional]` Trexler et al. 2017 (J Strength Cond Res), 235 NCAA Division I and II American football players measured by DXA: mean height-adjusted FFMI 23.7 ± 2.1 kg/m2; 62 players (26.4%) exceeded 25; 97.5th percentile 28.1; Division I 24.3 ± 1.8 vs Division II 23.4 ± 1.8. Authors conclude the natural limit in this population is above 25. Drug-free status rests on NCAA testing and was not verified per athlete; the sample is highly selected for size. — [Trexler et al. 2017, PubMed 27930454](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27930454/)
- `[TRIAL - cross-sectional]` A 2024 study of NCAA Division III football players: mean FFMI 23.50 ± 2.04, range 18.1-27.7. — [PMC11042886](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11042886/)

### Inferences
- Unit conversion of the McDonald model (mine): 20-25 lb = 9.1-11.3 kg (year 1); 10-12 lb = 4.5-5.4 kg (year 2); 5-6 lb = 2.3-2.7 kg (year 3); cumulative 35-43 lb = 15.9-19.5 kg over three years.
- The coaching models imply roughly three times the gain seen in the longest supervised trial retrieved (9.1-11.3 kg in 12 months vs 3.3 kg in about 9 months in Volek's best group). The models describe near-ideal training, eating and genetics; trial participants are average responders, often not in an energy surplus, and include women in Volek. Neither figure is "the" answer: the app should present the models as the ceiling and the trial data as what typical people measurably achieve.
- No primary study was found that validates the McDonald or Aragon year-by-year numbers. They should be labelled as experience-based estimates in the app.
- FFMI is fat-free mass (kg) divided by height (m) squared. Football-player data show 25 is exceeded by some drug-tested athletes, but these are large-framed, selected athletes; for a naturally slim 38-year-old, 25 is not a realistic target and the Kouri value is best used as "nobody should expect to exceed this naturally".

### Gaps
- Kouri 1995's exact sample numbers, mean FFMI values in users and non-users, and the paper's own wording on the 25 threshold were not confirmed from the abstract. Later critiques (including a claimed 2020 reappraisal) could not be identified by author.
- Hartman 2007's kg gains were not retrieved.
- Primary sources for the McDonald and Aragon models (their own articles) were not opened; only secondary summaries were seen. The exact figures in Helms's Muscle and Strength Pyramid books were not found.
- No trial longer than about 9 months measuring lean mass in novice men was retrieved, so years 2 and 3 have no trial anchor.
- No trial of lean-mass gain specifically in lean ("hardgainer") untrained men aged 35-45 in a deliberate energy surplus was found.
- Stronger By Science / MASS / Examine syntheses on rates of gain were not retrieved (budget exhausted).

## 6. Individual variability ("high and low responders") and what predicts it

### Takeaway
Variability between people is larger than any age effect at 38: in 585 people doing the same 12-week arm programme, biceps size change ranged from -2% to +59%. Around a quarter of trainees show little measurable size change on a given programme, while about one in seven are high responders. Predictors identified so far are biological (satellite cells, androgen receptor content), not age or sex, and none is usable by an app.

### Cited Findings
- `[TRIAL]` Hubal et al. 2005 (Med Sci Sports Exerc 37(6):964-972): 585 subjects (342 women, 243 men), 12 weeks of progressive training of the non-dominant arm, MRI biceps CSA. Size changes ranged from -2 to +59% (-0.4 to +13.6 cm2); 1RM gains 0 to +250%; isometric strength -32 to +149%. Men gained 2.5% more CSA than women; women had greater relative strength gains. — [Hubal et al. 2005, PubMed 15947721](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15947721/); range figures as quoted in [PainScience summary](https://www.painscience.com/biblio/genetic-differences-may-account-for-a-wide-range-of-strength-training-results.html)
- `[SECONDARY]` A later review describes about 6% of Hubal's subjects as showing practically no gain in muscle size. — [Räntilä et al. 2025, PMC12659766](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12659766/)
- `[TRIAL]` Ahtiainen et al. 2016: muscle size +4.8 ± 6.1% (range -11 to +30%), strength +21.1 ± 11.5% (range -8 to +60%); 14% and 12% classed as high responders for size and strength; using the upper 95% CI of non-training controls, 29% and 7% were low responders for size and strength; responses "varied extensively between the subjects regardless of subject's age and sex". — [Ahtiainen et al. 2016, PubMed 26767377](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26767377/)
- `[TRIAL]` Bamman et al. 2007 (J Appl Physiol 102(6):2232-9): 66 adults, 16 weeks; K-means clustering gave extreme responders (n = 17, mean myofibre growth +2,475 um2), modest responders (n = 32, +1,111 um2) and non-responders (n = 17, -16 um2). — [Bamman et al. 2007, PubMed 17395765](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17395765/)
- `[TRIAL]` Petrella et al. 2008 (same cohort): extreme responders had more satellite cells at baseline and were the only cluster to increase satellite-cell number robustly with training (+117%). — [Petrella et al. 2008, PubMed 18436694](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18436694/)
- `[TRIAL]` Morton et al. 2018: androgen receptor content, not circulating hormones, distinguished high from low responders (see section 4). — [Morton et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30356739/)
- `[TRIAL]` Räntilä et al. 2025: the standard deviation of individual responses exceeded zero for all variables, confirming true inter-individual heterogeneity beyond measurement error. A Journal of Physiology study (Lees et al.) reported negligible shared variance between training-induced gains in size and in strength. — [Räntilä et al. 2025, PMC12659766](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12659766/)

### Inferences
- The app should present outcomes as ranges, not point predictions, and should tell users early that two people on the same plan can differ several-fold in size gain.
- Because size and strength responses share little variance, a user who is getting stronger but not visibly bigger (or the reverse) is normal; the app should track both, plus measurements and photos, rather than a single metric.
- "Low responder" classifications are specific to one programme, one muscle and one measurement method over 12-24 weeks. Whether low responders improve with a changed stimulus (for example more volume) is plausible but the evidence was not retrieved in this session.
- Being "skinny" at baseline is not shown by any study retrieved here to predict being a low responder.

### Gaps
- No practical baseline predictor (body type, starting muscle mass, limb measurements) of response was found.
- Studies testing whether non-responders respond to higher volume or a different programme were not retrieved.
- Genetic (heritability) estimates for hypertrophy response were not researched.

## 7. How long before visible change

### Takeaway
Measurable muscle growth appears within about 3-4 weeks on imaging, though some of the earliest change is swelling rather than new tissue; clear growth is documented by 8-9 weeks. There is no research retrieved on when change becomes noticeable to other people, so any such timeline in the app is a coaching rule of thumb.

### Cited Findings
- `[TRIAL]` DeFreitas et al. 2011 (Eur J Appl Physiol): 25 sedentary men tested weekly over 8 weeks of high-intensity training; thigh CSA +13.9 cm2 (+9.60%) by the end; authors conclude hypertrophy "may occur early in a training program", with notable gains around weeks 3-4. — [DeFreitas et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-011-1905-4)
- `[TRIAL]` Seynnes et al. 2007 (J Appl Physiol): reported early skeletal-muscle hypertrophy and architectural change with high-intensity training; one later paper notes muscle size was measured as early as 10 days into training. Exact percentages were not in the extracts. — [Seynnes et al. 2007 (PDF)](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Early%20skeletal%20muscle%20hypertrophy%20and%20architectural%20changes%20in%20response%20to%20high-intensity%20resistance%20training.pdf)
- `[TRIAL/COMMENTARY]` Later work citing Damas et al. argues that increases in CSA 1-3 weeks into training "may be due to edema-induced muscle swelling" and that DeFreitas 2011 and Seynnes 2007 "could have overestimated the magnitude of muscle hypertrophy" because neither quantified swelling. — [Stock et al. 2017, Eur J Appl Physiol](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3792-9)
- `[TRIAL]` Lixandrão et al. 2016, **older adults**: significant vastus lateralis CSA increase only after 18 sessions / 9 weeks (+7.1%). — [Lixandrão et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26110345/)
- `[TRIAL]` Kerksick et al. 2009: +1.1 kg DXA lean mass in 35-50-year-old men at 8 weeks. — [Kerksick et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19387379/)
- `[TRIAL]` Kubo et al. 2010: strength up 29.6% at 2 months and 40.5% at 3 months, ahead of structural adaptation. — [Kubo et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19996769/)

### Inferences
- Sequence the app can describe honestly: weeks 1-3, strength and "pump"/swelling rise, little true tissue; weeks 4-9, growth measurable on imaging; by about 8-12 weeks, whole-body lean-mass change in the region of 1 kg is detectable by DXA in middle-aged novices.
- A gain of about 1-1.5 kg of lean mass spread over the whole body is generally too small to be obvious in clothes. `[COACHING RULE OF THUMB]` The user is likely to notice changes himself (tape measure, mirror, photos under the same lighting) before others comment; a commonly used expectation is that others notice somewhere after the 3-month mark. No study retrieved here supports a specific number of weeks.
- Strength progress is the earliest reliable feedback (tens of percent within 2-3 months) and is the right metric for the app to celebrate in the first 8 weeks, with photos and measurements every 4 weeks rather than weekly.

### Gaps
- No research on the time or amount of muscle gain needed before other people perceive a difference was found.
- Damas et al. 2016's own data (time point at which swelling no longer explains CSA change) were not retrieved; the critique is cited through a later paper.
- Exact early percentages from Seynnes 2007 were not confirmed.

## 8. Which muscle groups most drive a "muscular" look

### Takeaway
Perception research consistently points to the upper body and to leanness: perceived strength explained over 70% of the variance in ratings of men's bodily attractiveness, upper-body muscles were rated as mattering most, and broad shoulders relative to the waist are favoured, with one recent study finding diminishing returns beyond a shoulder-to-waist ratio of about 1.57 and body fat being the stronger predictor. Prioritising deltoids, lats, upper chest and arms for a slim frame is physique-coaching consensus consistent with that research, not something trials have tested.

### Cited Findings
- `[TRIAL]` Sell, Lukaszewski & Townsley 2017 (Proc R Soc B): raters' estimates of men's physical strength from body photographs explained over 70% of the variance in bodily attractiveness; adding height and leanness brought this to about 80%; there was no nonlinear effect, the strongest-looking men were the most attractive in all samples. The paper's title attributes this to cues of **upper-body** strength. — [Sell et al. 2017, PMC5745404](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5745404)
- `[TRIAL - survey]` Durkee et al. 2019 (Evolutionary Psychology 17(2)): responses from 503 women and 942 men on which muscles affect men's attractiveness and preferred sizes; both sexes rated upper-body muscles as important and lower-body muscles as less so; men's preferred muscle sizes were larger than women's. Abstract: women's preferences gave "only partial support" to the hypotheses; men tended to prefer larger upper-body muscles. — [Durkee et al. 2019, DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/a455161b2f44472a8271c6fb204db0a0)
- `[SECONDARY]` A Psychology Today summary of Durkee 2019 lists women's top five muscles by size preference as obliques, glutes, abdominals, biceps and shoulders, in that order. Not checked against the paper's tables. — [Psychology Today, June 2021](https://psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/love-digitally/202106/which-muscles-make-men-more-attractive)
- `[TRIAL]` Horvath 1981 (Archives of Sexual Behavior): both male and female raters judged broad shoulders attractive in male physiques. — [Horvath 1981](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/BF01542671.pdf)
- `[SECONDARY]` A recent study (reported 2025; 283 participants from China, Lithuania and the UK; journal given as Personality and Individual Differences): body-fat percentage was a stronger and more consistent predictor of male attractiveness than BMI or shoulder-to-waist ratio; a shoulder-to-waist ratio of about 1.57 was associated with the highest attractiveness, with little gain beyond it. Authors and exact statistics not confirmed. — [PsyPost report](https://psypost.org/what-is-the-most-attractive-body-fat-percentage-for-men-new-research-offers-an-answer/); [Psychology Today, July 2025](https://www.psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/finding-a-new-home/202507/what-makes-a-man-attractive-science-reveals-the-ideal-body)
- `[TRIAL]` Braun & Bryan (J Soc Pers Relat) examined men's waist-to-shoulder ratio and women's waist-to-hip ratio as determinants of desirability as a romantic partner; results were not available in the extract. — [Braun & Bryan (PDF)](https://www.unm.edu/%7Eabryan/articles/femalehipratio.pdf)
- `[TRIAL]` Swami & Tovée 2005 (Body Image) tested waist-to-chest ratio, BMI and waist-to-hip ratio as cues to male attractiveness in Britain and Malaysia; results not available in the extract. — [Swami & Tovée 2005](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/male-physical-attractiveness-in-britain-and-malaysia-a-cross-cult/)
- `[TRIAL]` Frederick & Haselton 2007, "Why is muscularity sexy? Tests of the fitness indicator hypothesis" (title only retrieved). — [Frederick & Haselton 2007, PubMed 17578932](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17578932/)

### Inferences
- These studies measure **attractiveness** ratings, mostly of photographs, which is a proxy for but not identical to "looks jacked". They still converge on three levers: apparent upper-body size, a wide shoulder-to-waist ratio, and low-to-moderate body fat.
- A lean ("skinny") 38-year-old already has the narrow waist, the half of the ratio that body fat controls. His ratio improves fastest by widening the top: lateral and rear deltoids and lats (shoulder and upper-back width), then chest and arms.
- `[COACHING RULE OF THUMB]` Physique-coaching priority order for a naturally slim frame, consistent with the above but not trial-tested: (1) deltoids, especially the lateral head; (2) lats and upper back; (3) chest with emphasis on the upper portion; (4) arms (triceps and biceps); then (5) legs, glutes and abdominals trained for balance, health and because the survey data rate abdominals, obliques and glutes highly. No trial retrieved compares prioritisation schemes for perceived muscularity.
- Durkee's secondary-reported ranking (obliques, glutes, abs first for women) is a caution against a pure "shoulders and arms only" programme: midsection definition is mostly a leanness outcome, which a slim man already has, and glutes need lower-body training.
- Men preferred larger muscles than women did (Durkee), so the user's own target ("jacked") may exceed what others rate as most attractive. The app should ask which audience the user cares about rather than assume.

### Gaps
- No study was found measuring which muscle groups most influence third-party judgements of "muscular" (as opposed to "attractive").
- Hughes & Gallup 2003 (shoulder-to-hip ratio) was not found; Braun & Bryan and Swami & Tovée results were not retrieved.
- The often-quoted "1.6 golden ratio" for shoulders to waist appeared only on a fitness blog and has no peer-reviewed support in what was retrieved; the 1.57 figure above is from press coverage of one study.
- Physique-coaching literature (Helms, Henselmans, Stronger By Science) on prioritisation for slim frames was not directly retrieved; the priority order above is stated as consensus from general knowledge and must be labelled a rule of thumb.

## 9. Minimum effective dose and adherence

### Takeaway
The evidence-based minimum for growth is small: at least 4 hard sets per muscle group per week with multi-joint lifts in the 6-15RM range, with more growth up to at least 10 weekly sets, and weekly volume mattering more than how it is split. Dropout is the dominant risk: in a 5,240-member gym cohort about 63% had quit by 3 months and about 96% by 12 months. A programme short enough to keep doing beats an optimal one that is abandoned.

### Cited Findings
- `[META - narrative]` Iversen et al. 2021 (Sports Medicine 51(10):2079-2095), aimed at the general public with limited time: prioritise bilateral, multi-joint movements through full range of motion, with at least 4 weekly sets per muscle group at 6-15RM (15-40 repetitions acceptable if taken to volitional failure); weekly volume matters more than frequency; supersets, drop sets and rest-pause roughly halve training time while maintaining volume; restrict warm-up to exercise-specific sets; stretch only if flexibility is a goal. — [Iversen et al. 2021, PubMed 34125411](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/)
- `[META]` Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (J Sports Sci), 15 studies / 34 treatment groups: graded dose-response between weekly sets and muscle growth, with gains increasing up to 10+ weekly sets per muscle group and too little data to conclude beyond that. — [Schoenfeld et al. 2017 (PDF)](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Dose-response%20relationship%20between%20weekly%20resistance%20training%20volume%20and%20increases%20in%20muscle%20mass%20-%20A%20systematic%20review%20and%20metaanalysis.pdf)
- `[SECONDARY]` Earlier meta-analyses found training frequency had a negligible effect on hypertrophy once weekly volume was equated. — [Biolayne REPS, issue 31](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-31/the-king-of-volume-metas/)
- `[META]` Benito et al. 2020: sets per workout was negatively associated with muscle gain in men. — [Benito et al. 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/4/1285/xml)
- `[COHORT]` Steele et al. 2022 (Res Q Exerc Sport 94:913-930): training records of 14,690 people doing one session per week of single sets to momentary failure on six exercises, for up to about 6.8 years; strength gains were substantial, plateaued at around one year and were then largely maintained. Strength only; muscle size was not measured. — [Steele et al., SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/69)
- `[META]` Androulakis-Korakakis et al. (Sports Medicine), resistance-trained men: a single set of 6-12 repetitions at 70-85% 1RM, 2-3 times per week for 8-12 weeks, was sufficient to increase squat and bench-press 1RM. Strength outcome in already-trained men. — [Androulakis-Korakakis et al., USC record](https://research.usc.edu.au/esploro/outputs/preprint/The-minimum-effective-training-dose-required/99533806302621)
- `[COHORT]` Sperandei, Vieira & Reis 2016 (J Sci Med Sport), 5,240 members of a fitness centre in Rio de Janeiro. `[SECONDARY]` dropout was 63% by three months and 96% by 12 months. Age, previous physical-activity level, initial BMI and motivations (weight loss, hypertrophy, health, aesthetics) were associated with risk of quitting. — [Sperandei et al. 2016, Western Sydney University record](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/adherence-to-physical-activity-in-an-unsupervised-setting-the-cas/); dropout figures as quoted in [a later open-access review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11332-020-00626-6)

### Inferences
- A defensible beginner default for the app: 2-3 sessions per week of about 45-60 minutes built on compound lifts, giving every major muscle group at least 4 and working toward about 10 hard sets per week, with extra sets directed to the priority muscles in section 8. The session count and length are my synthesis; the set numbers are from Iversen and Schoenfeld.
- An explicit "minimum viable session" (one hard set per exercise, about 6 exercises, once or twice a week) is evidence-supported for maintaining and even building strength (Steele; Androulakis-Korakakis) and gives the app a fallback for bad weeks that keeps the habit alive. Its effect on muscle size in novices is less certain.
- Since about two-thirds of new gym members quit within 3 months, the first 12 weeks should be designed around adherence (short sessions, early strength wins, low soreness) rather than maximal stimulus. This coincides with the period when tendons are catching up (section 3) and visible change is still small (section 7), so expectation-setting matters most here.
- Sperandei found aesthetics and hypertrophy motivations were among the variables associated with dropout risk, but the direction of each association was not in the extract; do not state in the app that aesthetic motivation increases or decreases dropout without checking the paper.

### Gaps
- Evidence on what specifically keeps resistance-training beginners adherent (session length, enjoyment, autonomy, supervision, habit-formation frequency) could not be searched; the search budget was exhausted before these queries ran. Nothing is asserted here on enjoyment or habit formation.
- The Sperandei 12-month and 3-month percentages come from a secondary citation; the widely repeated "3.7% still training at 12 months" figure was seen only on a non-academic site.
- Percentage hypertrophy differences per additional weekly set from Schoenfeld 2017 were not retrieved; more recent dose-response meta-regressions (for example 2024-2025 work by Swinton, Schoenfeld, Murphy and by Pelland and colleagues) were not opened.
- No minimum-dose study with a hypertrophy outcome in untrained men aged 35-45 was found.

## 10. What a realistic 3-month, 1-year and 3-year outcome looks like for a lean 38-year-old novice

### Takeaway
Every number in this section is my derivation from the sources above, not a study result. Trial data anchor the 3-month figure reasonably well (about 1-3 kg of lean mass and large strength gains); the 1-year figure spans roughly 3 kg (trial-anchored) to 9-11 kg (best-case coaching model); the 3-year figure rests on coaching models only (up to about 16-19.5 kg cumulative), bounded by an FFMI of about 25.

### Cited Findings
- `[TRIAL]` 8 weeks, men 35-50: +1.1 ± 1.9 kg lean mass. — [Kerksick et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19387379/)
- `[META]` Pooled across 111 studies in men: +1.53 kg (95% CI 1.30-1.76). — [Benito et al. 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/4/1285/xml)
- `[TRIAL]` About 9 months, untrained men and women: +3.3 ± 1.5 kg in the best group, +1.8 to +2.3 kg in the others. — [Volek et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24015719/)
- `[TRIAL]` Strength +29.6% at 2 months and +40.5% at 3 months (isometric knee extension). — [Kubo et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19996769/)
- `[COHORT]` On a once-weekly minimal dose, strength gains plateau at about one year. — [Steele et al.](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/69)
- `[COACHING RULE OF THUMB]` McDonald: 20-25 lb in year 1, 10-12 lb in year 2, 5-6 lb in year 3. Aragon: 1-1.5% of body weight per month for beginners. — [RNT Fitness summary](https://www.rntfitness.co.uk/realistic-rates-muscle-gain/)
- `[TRIAL - cross-sectional]` FFMI of about 25 as the observed upper bound in non-steroid-users. — [Kouri et al. 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7496846/)
- `[TRIAL]` Size response on an identical programme ranged from -2% to +59%. — [Hubal et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15947721/)

### Inferences
All figures below are illustrative. Worked examples assume a hypothetical man of 65 kg, 1.78 m and 12% body fat (the real user's numbers are unknown): fat-free mass 57.2 kg, FFMI 57.2 / 1.78^2 = 18.1.

- **3 months.** Trial-anchored: about 1-1.5 kg of lean mass (Kerksick, Benito). Aragon model for 65 kg: 0.65-1.0 kg per month, so about 2-3 kg. Honest app range: roughly 1-3 kg of lean mass, strength up by tens of percent on main lifts. Visual: he will see it in photos and measurements (shoulders, arms, chest); clothes fit slightly tighter across the shoulders; most other people will not yet comment. He will not look "jacked".
- **1 year.** Trial-anchored floor: about 3 kg (Volek, 9 months, mixed sex, no deliberate surplus). Model ceiling: 9.1-11.3 kg (McDonald). A planning range of about 4-9 kg for a consistent, well-fed male novice is my judgment between those anchors. In the worked example, +4 kg gives FFMI 19.3 and +9 kg gives FFMI 20.9. Visual: clearly athletic and visibly different from baseline in a T-shirt; noticeably broader shoulders and fuller arms and chest; "fit and muscular" to most observers, not yet "jacked" by gym standards.
- **3 years.** Model-only: cumulative 15.9-19.5 kg (McDonald) would take the worked example to FFMI 23.1-24.2, close to the Kouri upper bound, which implies the full model figure is a genetic-best-case outcome for a slim-framed man. A more cautious planning range of about 8-14 kg (FFMI roughly 20.6-22.5 in the example) is my judgment. Visual: this is the timescale on which "jacked" in everyday terms (obviously muscular in clothes, pronounced shoulder-to-waist taper at low body fat) becomes realistic for an average-to-good responder; a low responder may reach only the lower end.
- **Caveats the app should show with every projection:** (1) individual response varies several-fold (Hubal, Ahtiainen); (2) age 38 is not expected to reduce these numbers (Kerksick, Benito, Lowndes); (3) the 1-year and 3-year figures assume he is still training, and most beginners are not (Sperandei); (4) body-weight gain will exceed lean-mass gain because some fat and water come with it.
- The arithmetic: 1.78^2 = 3.168; (57.2 + 4) / 3.168 = 19.3; (57.2 + 9) / 3.168 = 20.9; (57.2 + 8) / 3.168 = 20.6; (57.2 + 14) / 3.168 = 22.5; (57.2 + 15.9) / 3.168 = 23.1; (57.2 + 19.5) / 3.168 = 24.2. Model "muscle" gains are treated as fat-free mass for this purpose, which slightly overstates FFMI.

### Gaps
- No trial retrieved follows natural novice men beyond about 9 months with body-composition measurement; years 2-3 are extrapolation from coaching models.
- No study links a given lean-mass gain or FFMI to being perceived as "jacked"; the visual descriptions are judgment.
- The user's actual height, weight and body-fat level are needed to replace the worked example.
- The planning ranges (4-9 kg at 1 year, 8-14 kg at 3 years) are my interpolations and should be presented in the app as estimates, not as published figures.
